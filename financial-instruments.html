Foundations · Deep Reference

Financial Instruments

What actually trades in the markets — from bills to options — organised by the capital-structure ladder

The previous article mapped how the market is divided; this one works through the instruments that live inside it. Every instrument is a claim of some kind, and the single thread running through all of them is seniority — who gets paid first when things go wrong. Learn that ladder and the whole zoo of instruments, from a Treasury bill to a convertible bond to a put option, falls into order.

Objective

Having mapped the market's structure, we now turn to what actually changes hands within it. There are a great many financial instruments, and a beginner meets them as a bewildering list — bills, notes, bonds, loans, shares, options, swaps. The goal here is to replace that list with a structure. Almost every instrument is either a form of lending (debt), a form of ownership (equity), a blend of the two (hybrid), or a contract deriving its value from something else (a derivative) — and the debt-to-equity instruments line up on a single ladder of seniority.

The capital-structure ladder — the spine of this whole article

When a company is healthy, these distinctions feel abstract. When it fails, they are everything: they decide, in strict order, who gets paid from whatever value remains. Higher on the ladder means safer and lower-yielding; lower means riskier and higher-yielding. This one picture ties bonds, loans, preferred shares, and common shares together.

1 · Senior secured debt — first-lien loans, revolving credit. Backed by collateral, paid first. Safest, lowest return.
2 · Senior unsecured debt — second-lien loans, senior bonds. No collateral, but still ahead of most.
3 · Subordinated / junior debt — high-yield bonds, mezzanine. Paid after senior debt; higher risk and yield.
4 · Preferred equity — preferred shares. Paid after all debt, before common. A debt-equity hybrid.
5 · Common equity — common shares. The residual claim — paid last, unlimited upside, first to be wiped out.

Keep this ladder in mind throughout. As each instrument appears, its place on the ladder tells you most of what you need to know about its risk and return before you learn a single other detail. Derivatives, funds, currencies, and commodities sit outside the ladder — they are contracts or wrappers rather than claims on a single company — but even they are best understood by contrast with it.

Instrument 01 · Debt · Short-term

Money-market instruments

The short-term, high-safety plumbing of the financial system

Money-market instruments are the shortest and safest form of debt — short-term IOUs that mature in under a year and are prized above all for liquidity and capital preservation. This is where governments, banks, and corporations manage day-to-day cash: parking a surplus for a few weeks, or borrowing to bridge a short gap. Returns are low because risk is low; the whole point is safety and access, not yield.

How they work — the discount mechanism

Many money-market instruments don't pay a traditional interest coupon. Instead they are sold on a discount basis: you buy below face value and receive the full face value at maturity, and the difference is your return. Buy a Treasury bill for $980 and collect $1,000 thirteen weeks later — the $20 is your interest, expressed as an annualised yield. This mechanism is simple, transparent, and suited to very short holding periods.

The main instruments

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term government debt — effectively free of default risk, highly liquid, auctioned regularly (e.g. 13- and 26-week bills). Central banks buy and sell them to implement monetary policy, which makes them the anchor of the entire money market. Commercial paper is the corporate equivalent: short-term, unsecured debt issued by large, creditworthy companies to fund immediate needs like payroll or inventory — higher-yielding than T-bills precisely because it is unsecured. Certificates of deposit (CDs) are time deposits at a bank paying a fixed rate; a negotiable CD can be sold on the secondary market before maturity. Repurchase agreements (repos) are the market's short-term collateralised loan: one party sells a security and agrees to buy it back shortly at a slightly higher price — the difference is effectively interest, and the security is the collateral. Bankers' acceptances are short-term trade IOUs guaranteed by a bank, widely used in international trade to create trust between parties who don't know each other.

📊 Scale & role
Money markets are enormous and systemically vital — in the US they account for roughly one-third of all credit. Maturities run from overnight to one year, and the market is mostly OTC and wholesale (large institutional tickets), benchmarked historically to LIBOR and now to rates like SOFR.
Where it sits on the ladder

These are debt instruments, so they sit near the top of the seniority ladder — and T-bills, backed by a government, sit above any corporate claim entirely. This is why a money-market fund is treated as a near-cash holding: the combination of short maturity, high credit quality, and seniority makes price movements tiny.

⚠ What to watch
Safe is not risk-free. Commercial paper is unsecured — in a crisis (2008), the commercial-paper market froze and even "safe" money-market funds came under stress. Credit quality of the issuer still matters.

Yield ≈ compensation for the tiny risk taken. If a money-market instrument offers a notably higher yield, ask what extra risk you're being paid for.

Reinvestment risk. Because maturities are so short, you're constantly rolling over at whatever the new (possibly lower) rate is.

Sources: CFI — Money Market; IMF F&D — What Are Money Markets?; ScienceDirect — Money Market Instruments; AFP — Money Markets.

Instrument 02 · Debt · Long-term

Bonds (fixed income)

A tradable loan: lend now, collect coupons, get your principal back at maturity

A bond is the fundamental long-term debt instrument — essentially a tradable loan. An investor lends a fixed sum to an issuer (a government or company); in return the issuer pays periodic interest and promises to return the principal on a set future date. Because the payment stream is defined in advance, bonds are called fixed income, and because a bondholder is a creditor, they rank above all equity on the capital-structure ladder.

The anatomy of a bond
Face / par value — the amount repaid at maturity (typically $1,000, quoted on a base of 100). Not the same as the price.
Coupon rate — the annual interest, as a % of face value. A 5% coupon on $1,000 pays $50 a year.
Maturity — the date the principal is repaid and payments stop. Ranges from months to 100 years.
Price — what the bond trades at, quoted as a % of par. "98" means 98% of face = $980 (a discount); above 100 is a premium.
Yield to maturity (YTM) — the total annualised return if held to maturity, accounting for price, coupons, and time. The key comparison metric.
Indenture — the legal contract setting out all the bond's terms and protections.
The one relationship you must internalise
Bond prices move inversely to interest rates

This is the single most important fact in fixed income. If you hold a bond paying a 5% coupon and market rates rise to 7%, no one will pay full price for your 5% bond when they can buy a new one at 7% — so your bond's price falls until its yield matches the market. Conversely, when rates fall, existing higher-coupon bonds become more valuable and their prices rise. Rates up, prices down; rates down, prices up. How much the price moves for a given rate change is called duration — the subject of the valuation article.

The main types

By issuer and structure: Government bonds — in the US, split by maturity into bills (under 1 year), notes (2–10 years), and bonds (20–30 years); generally the safest, with Treasuries the global benchmark. Municipal bonds — issued by cities and states, often tax-advantaged. Corporate bonds — issued by companies, higher-yielding, ranked by credit rating from investment-grade down to high-yield ("junk"). STRIPS — Treasuries with their coupons "stripped" off, so the principal and each coupon trade separately as zero-coupon instruments (bought at a discount, no interim payments). Floating-rate notes (FRNs) — bonds whose coupon resets with market rates rather than staying fixed.

A key issuer option: callable bonds

Some bonds are callable — the issuer can repay them early, after an initial protected period. Issuers call bonds when it suits them, which is typically when rates have fallen and they can refinance more cheaply — exactly when the investor would have preferred to keep the higher coupon. A callable bond therefore carries extra risk for the investor and usually offers a higher yield to compensate.

📊 Why investors hold bonds
Four roles: income (predictable coupons), capital preservation (principal returned at maturity, especially with governments), diversification (bonds often move differently from equities, lowering portfolio volatility), and market signalling (bond yields reveal the market's expectations for rates and growth).
⚠ What to watch
Interest-rate risk. Rising rates push bond prices down; the longer the maturity/duration, the bigger the hit.

Credit risk. The issuer can default. This is what separates a Treasury from a high-yield corporate — and what the credit rating measures.

Call risk. A callable bond can be taken away just when its high coupon is most valuable to you.

Inflation risk. Fixed coupons lose real value if inflation rises — a fixed 4% is poor if inflation runs at 6%.

Price ≠ par. A bond trading at a premium or discount changes your real return (YTM), which is not the same as the coupon rate.

Sources: Raymond James — Bond Basics; CFI — Bonds; Britannica Money — Bond Market Basics; Vanguard — What Is a Bond.

Instrument 03 · Debt · Private

Loans & credit

The most senior debt of all — private, negotiated, and first in line

Loans are debt too, but of a different character from bonds. A bond is a security sold to many investors in a public market; a loan is a privately negotiated agreement, historically between a company and a bank (or a group of them). Crucially, loans usually sit at the very top of the capital-structure ladder — they are the most senior claim, the first to be repaid, and often secured by collateral. Where a bondholder lends at arm's length, a lender negotiates terms directly and holds more control.

The two core loan structures

A term loan is a lump sum borrowed and repaid over a set schedule. It splits into "Term Loan A" (amortising, typically held by banks) and "Term Loan B" (bullet repayment, held by institutional investors like credit funds and CLOs). A revolving credit facility ("revolver") works like a corporate credit card: the company draws down and repays as needed, up to a limit, paying a fee on the undrawn portion. The revolver is typically the first debt repaid and the most senior of all.

Seniority within debt — the lien structure

Not all debt is equal, and the ordering within the debt layers is precise. First-lien debt has the first claim on the borrower's collateral — the safest position, lowest yield. Second-lien debt sits behind it, with a claim only on what's left after first-lien is satisfied — riskier, higher yield. Below that comes unsecured and subordinated debt. A key rule of thumb: loans are almost always senior to bonds from the same company, structured deliberately so loans are repaid first in a bankruptcy.

Leveraged loans — the private-equity fuel

A leveraged loan is a loan to a below-investment-grade (riskier, more indebted) company — typically to finance a leveraged buyout, acquisition, or refinancing. Despite the "risky borrower" label, the loan itself is relatively well-protected: it is senior, secured by collateral, and sits at the top of the structure, making it the safest slice of a risky company's debt. Leveraged loans are floating-rate (priced as a spread over a benchmark like SOFR), which makes them attractive when rates rise — unlike fixed-rate bonds, their income goes up with rates.

📊 Syndication — how billion-dollar loans get funded
No single bank lends billions alone. In syndication, a lead ("arranger") bank underwrites the full amount, then sells portions to a group of other banks and institutional investors. This spreads the risk and is how large financings actually get done. The institutional slices (Term Loan B) are bought by CLOs, credit funds, and insurers — and increasingly by private credit funds lending directly, outside the banking system entirely.
Covenants — the lender's controls

Loans come with covenants — contractual rules protecting the lender. Maintenance covenants must be met continuously (e.g. keep debt/EBITDA below a limit, tested quarterly); breaching one is an event of default. Incurrence covenants only bite when the borrower takes a specific action (like taking on more debt). Since the 2010s, many institutional loans have become "covenant-lite" — lacking maintenance covenants — which gives borrowers more room but leaves lenders less protected, a structural risk worth watching.

⚠ What to watch
Seniority is the whole point. A leveraged loan to a shaky company can still be safe because it's first-lien secured — but check where in the lien structure you actually sit.

Floating rate cuts both ways. Income rises with rates, but so does the borrower's interest burden — which can push a weak borrower toward default.

Cov-lite = less early warning. Without maintenance covenants, problems surface later, when there's less value left to recover.

Liquidity. Syndicated loans have a secondary market, but private-credit loans generally do not — you may be locked in.

Sources: Wall Street Prep — Leveraged Finance; Ryan O'Connell, CFA — Leveraged Finance; PitchBook — Leveraged Loan Primer; Corvid Partners — Leveraged Loans.

Instrument 04 · Equity · Ownership

Equity: common vs. preferred shares

Ownership, not lending — the residual claim, and its quieter cousin

Equity is fundamentally different from everything above it. A bondholder or lender is a creditor — owed a defined sum. A shareholder is an owner — entitled to whatever is left after everyone else is paid. This is the residual claim: unlimited upside if the company thrives, but last in line if it fails. Within equity, though, there are two quite different instruments — common and preferred shares — and the difference between them is one of the most common things beginners find confusing.

Common stock — ownership in full

Common stock is what most people mean by "a share." It carries three defining features. First, voting rights — typically one vote per share, used to elect the board of directors and approve major decisions; this is the owner's voice in how the company is run. Second, a residual claim — common shareholders are paid last, after creditors and preferred shareholders, receiving whatever assets remain in a liquidation (often nothing). Third, variable dividends — payments are not guaranteed; they rise, fall, or stop entirely with the company's fortunes. In exchange for bearing the most risk, common holders capture the most upside: when a company grows, the value of its common shares can rise without limit.

Preferred stock — equity that behaves like a bond

Preferred stock is a hybrid, and the cleanest way to understand it is as "equity that acts like a bond." Preferred shareholders usually give up the vote, but in return they get financial priority. Their dividends are fixed and must be paid before any dividend to common shareholders. In a liquidation, they rank ahead of common shareholders (though still behind all debt). And their return is essentially capped — like a bond's coupon — at the preferred dividend, without the unlimited upside of common. Because of this fixed, priority income, preferred shares suit income-focused, more risk-averse investors, while common suits those seeking growth and willing to bear volatility.

Common vs. preferred — the difference in one view

Common: votes · variable/uncertain dividend · paid last · unlimited upside · higher volatility · for growth.
Preferred: usually no vote · fixed priority dividend · paid before common (after debt) · capped return · lower volatility · for income.

The mental shortcut: common is pure ownership; preferred is a bond-like claim wearing an equity label. This is exactly why preferred sits on rung 4 of the ladder — above common, below all debt.

Varieties of preferred

Preferred shares come in flavours worth knowing. Cumulative preferred accumulates any missed dividends — the company must pay all arrears before common holders see a cent; non-cumulative does not. Convertible preferred can be exchanged for a set number of common shares, giving some of the upside. And in venture capital, liquidation preferences are central: a "1x" preference returns the investor their money first before common holders share the rest; a "2x" returns double. Participating preferred takes its preference and then shares in the remainder; non-participating takes the greater of the two. These terms decide who actually gets what when a startup is sold.

📊 Where equity sits on the ladder
Both types are below all debt, but preferred (rung 4) ranks above common (rung 5). The full waterfall in a liquidation: secured lenders → unsecured/senior bondholders → subordinated debt → preferred shareholders → common shareholders. Equity holders only receive anything once every creditor is fully paid — which is why equity is the highest-risk, highest-expected-return part of the structure.
⚠ What to watch
Common = last in line. In a bankruptcy, common shareholders are usually wiped out entirely. The upside is unlimited, but so is the risk to your capital.

Preferred is rate-sensitive. Because it behaves like a fixed-income instrument, preferred stock's price falls when interest rates rise — much like a bond.

Preferred's upside is capped. You get the fixed dividend and priority, but you don't share in the company's growth the way common holders do.

Voting matters in control fights. Common's vote is worth little day-to-day but decisive in takeovers, proxy battles, and board elections.

"Preferred" ≠ "better." It's a different risk/return profile, not a superior one — safer income, less growth.

Sources: Chase — Common vs Preferred; Harvard Business Services — Common vs Preferred; Lumen — Common & Preferred Stock; Diffen — Common vs Preferred (liquidation preferences).

Instrument 05 · Between debt & equity

Hybrids

Instruments that deliberately sit between the rungs of the ladder

Not every instrument is cleanly debt or equity. Hybrids are designed to blend features of both, sitting between the rungs of the capital-structure ladder — offering some of the safety of debt with some of the upside of equity, or vice versa. They exist because issuers and investors sometimes want a risk/return profile that neither pure debt nor pure equity provides. Two matter most: convertible bonds and (already met) preferred shares.

Convertible bonds — debt with an equity option

A convertible bond is a corporate bond that the holder can convert into a predetermined number of the company's common shares. It starts life as debt — paying a coupon, ranking above equity — but carries an embedded option to become equity if the share price rises enough. The appeal is asymmetric: if the company does well, the holder converts and captures the stock's upside; if it does poorly, the holder keeps collecting the coupon and gets principal back at maturity, with the downside protection of a bond. In exchange for that attractive combination, convertibles pay a lower coupon than an ordinary bond from the same issuer — the investor gives up some yield to buy the conversion option.

Why companies issue convertibles

Convertibles let a company borrow at a lower interest rate than straight debt (because the conversion option is valuable to buyers), while deferring the dilution of issuing shares directly. They are especially popular with younger, high-growth companies that want cheap financing now and expect their share price to rise. The trade-off: if the stock soars and holders convert, existing shareholders are diluted after all.

Mezzanine — the gap-filler in deals

Mezzanine financing sits between senior debt and equity in a company's funding structure — subordinated debt that often comes with equity "kickers" (warrants or options to buy shares). It is riskier than senior debt, so it carries a high interest rate, and it is common in leveraged buyouts and growth financings where a company needs more capital than senior lenders will provide but the owners don't want to give up pure equity. On the ladder, mezzanine sits below senior and second-lien debt but above preferred and common equity.

Preferred shares, revisited

The other great hybrid — preferred stock — was covered in the equity section, but it belongs conceptually here too: it is legally equity, but economically behaves like a bond (fixed priority dividend, capped return, rate-sensitive price). Together, convertibles, mezzanine, and preferred form a continuous bridge across the gap between pure lending and pure ownership.

⚠ What to watch
Convertibles are two instruments in one. You're holding a bond and a stock option — value both. The bond floor protects you; the option gives upside.

Lower coupon is the price of the option. Don't compare a convertible's yield to a straight bond's without accounting for the conversion right you're buying.

Dilution risk for shareholders. If you own the common stock, outstanding convertibles are potential future shares that can dilute you.

Mezzanine is high-risk debt. Its high yield reflects its subordinated position — in a default, it's paid only after all senior debt.

Hybrids are complex. Their blended nature makes them harder to value and sometimes less liquid than pure debt or equity.

Sources: CFI — Convertible Bonds; Lumen — Hybrid Securities; PitchBook — Mezzanine & Capital Structure; Wall Street Prep — Capital Structure.

Instrument 06 · Wrappers

Funds (mutual funds & ETFs)

Not a new claim, but a basket — pooled access to many instruments at once

Funds are different in kind from everything so far. A bond or a share is a direct claim on a single issuer; a fund is a wrapper that pools many investors' money to buy a basket of underlying instruments. You don't buy a new type of claim — you buy a slice of a professionally-assembled portfolio. Funds exist to solve two problems for ordinary investors: diversification (owning hundreds of securities at once, impossible to assemble individually with small sums) and access to professional management.

The active vs. passive divide

The deepest distinction among funds is the strategy. An actively managed fund hires managers who research securities and try to beat the market — charging higher fees for that effort. A passively managed (index) fund simply tracks an index like the S&P 500, holding whatever the index holds, with no stock-picking — which makes it far cheaper. The long-running debate: most active managers fail to beat their benchmark after fees over time, which is why low-cost passive funds have captured enormous market share. This is the same active/passive split that defines the asset-management industry.

Mutual funds vs. ETFs — the structural difference
Mutual fund — priced once per day after market close at its net asset value (NAV). You buy/sell directly with the fund company at that day's NAV. Often actively managed; can have minimums and sales loads.
ETF (exchange-traded fund) — trades on an exchange all day like a stock, with a live price. Usually passive/index, lower fees, and publishes its holdings daily (high transparency). More flexible and tax-efficient for most investors.
ETN (exchange-traded note) — looks like an ETF but is actually a debt obligation of a bank, not a basket of assets. It carries the issuer's credit risk — if the bank fails, you can lose out even if the tracked index did fine.
ETP (exchange-traded product) — the umbrella term covering ETFs, ETNs, and commodity pools.
📊 Why the structure matters
The headline difference is intraday trading: an ETF can be bought and sold any moment the market is open, at a known price; a mutual fund settles once daily at NAV. Combined with generally lower fees, daily transparency, and tax efficiency, this is why ETFs have grown so fast. But an ETN is not a fund at all — it's a bank's IOU, so it adds issuer credit risk that a true ETF does not have.
Where funds sit on the ladder

A fund has no place of its own on the seniority ladder — its risk is simply the blended risk of whatever it holds. A government-bond fund is near the top; an emerging-market equity fund is near the bottom; a balanced fund sits in between. The wrapper doesn't change the seniority of the underlying claims; it just packages them.

⚠ What to watch
Fees compound. A 1% annual fee sounds small but erodes decades of returns dramatically. For index exposure, the cheapest fund tracking the same index usually wins.

Active rarely beats passive after fees. Paying up for active management is paying for a result most managers don't deliver over the long run.

ETN ≠ ETF. An ETN carries bank credit risk. Check whether a product is a genuine fund (holds assets) or a note (an IOU).

Know what's inside. A fund's risk is its holdings' risk. "Diversified" doesn't mean safe if everything it holds is correlated.

Tracking error. An index fund won't perfectly match its index — fees and mechanics create small gaps.

Sources: Invesco — Mutual Funds vs ETFs; FINRA — Exchange-Traded Products; NerdWallet — ETF vs Mutual Fund.

Instrument 07 · Contracts · Derived value

Derivatives

Contracts whose value comes from something else — the tools of hedging and leverage

Derivatives are a different species entirely. A share or a bond is a claim with value of its own; a derivative has no intrinsic value — its worth is derived from an underlying asset or reference price (a stock, a commodity, an interest rate, a currency). A derivative is a contract about that underlying, and it exists for three purposes: to hedge (transfer unwanted risk to someone willing to bear it), to speculate (bet on a price move with leverage), and to arbitrage (exploit pricing gaps). There are four fundamental types.

Forwards — the simplest derivative

A forward is a private, customised agreement between two parties to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date. Both sides are obligated to transact. A wheat farmer and a bakery might agree today on a price for grain to be delivered after harvest — locking in certainty for both, whatever the market does later. Because forwards are bespoke and traded OTC, they carry counterparty risk (the other side might not honour it) and can be illiquid.

Futures — the standardised forward

A future is a forward made standardised and exchange-traded. The contract size, quality, and dates are fixed by the exchange, and a clearing house stands behind every trade — virtually eliminating counterparty risk. Futures are marked to market daily: gains and losses are settled each day through a margin account, rather than all at once at expiry. This makes them highly liquid and transparent, which is why an airline hedging jet fuel or a fund speculating on oil uses futures rather than forwards. Like forwards, both parties are obligated to fulfil the contract.

Options — the right, not the obligation

Options are where derivatives get their asymmetry. An option gives the holder the right, but not the obligation, to buy or sell the underlying at a set price (the strike) before or at expiry. This one-sided nature is the whole point.

Call option — the right to buy at the strike. You buy a call when you expect the price to rise. Maximum loss is the premium paid; upside is theoretically unlimited.
Put option — the right to sell at the strike. You buy a put when you expect the price to fall, or to insure a holding against a drop.
Premium — the price paid for the option. For the buyer, this is the maximum possible loss. For the writer (seller), the premium is the maximum gain — but the potential loss can be large or unlimited.
Strike price — the fixed price at which the option can be exercised.
American vs. European — American options can be exercised any time before expiry; European only at expiry.
In / at / out of the money — whether exercising now would be profitable (in), break-even (at), or unprofitable (out).

The asymmetry is what makes options so useful: a call buyer risks only the premium but captures unlimited upside — a leveraged bet with a capped, known downside. Options are often cash-settled (the difference is paid in cash) rather than resulting in physical delivery of the underlying.

Swaps — exchanging cash-flow streams

A swap is an agreement between two parties to exchange streams of cash flows over time. The most common is the interest-rate swap: one party pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate, while the other does the reverse. A company with floating-rate debt that fears rising rates can swap into fixed, gaining certainty; a bank with fixed-rate mortgage income can swap into floating to match its funding. Other swaps exchange currencies (currency swaps), credit risk (credit default swaps), or commodity exposures. Swaps are overwhelmingly OTC and bespoke, which is why they dominate the OTC derivatives market.

📊 Leverage — the double edge
Derivatives let you control a large exposure for a small upfront outlay (a premium or margin). That leverage magnifies both gains and losses: a small move in the underlying can produce an outsized percentage return — or wipe out your stake. This is what makes derivatives powerful for hedging and dangerous for undisciplined speculation. The underlying notional controlled can be many times the cash committed.
⚠ What to watch
Obligation vs. right. Forwards and futures obligate both sides; options give the buyer a right. This distinction changes the entire risk profile.

Writing options is dangerous. Selling (writing) a call can expose you to unlimited loss for a small premium. Buyers risk only the premium; writers can risk far more.

Leverage cuts both ways. The same small outlay that magnifies gains magnifies losses — position sizing is everything.

Counterparty risk in OTC. Forwards and swaps depend on the other side honouring the deal; futures largely remove this via clearing.

Hedging vs. speculating. The same instrument can reduce risk (hedging an exposure you have) or create it (a naked bet). Know which you're doing.

Time decay (options). An option loses value as expiry approaches, all else equal — you can be right on direction and still lose if you're too early.

Sources: Fiveable — Derivatives; FINRA — Options; HedgeStar — Hedging with Derivatives; Capital City Training — Forwards, Futures, Options, Swaps.

Instrument 08 · Currencies

Currencies (FX)

The largest market on earth — trading one currency against another

The foreign-exchange (FX) market is the largest and most liquid market in the world, trading currencies against one another around the clock. Unlike a stock, a currency has no issuer paying you a return — you profit (or lose) purely from the exchange rate moving. FX is traded in pairs (EUR/USD, GBP/CHF), because the price of one currency is always expressed in terms of another. It serves two very different crowds: businesses managing real cross-border cash flows, and speculators betting on rate moves.

Spot and forward FX

Spot FX is the immediate exchange of one currency for another at the current rate — what happens when you convert money at a bank. Forward FX is the single most important tool for hedging currency risk: a company that will receive a foreign-currency payment in three months can lock in today's rate with a forward, removing the uncertainty of where the exchange rate will be. A UK exporter selling to a US buyer, with costs in pounds but revenue in dollars, uses FX forwards so a falling dollar doesn't erase its profit. Beyond these, currency swaps, FX futures, and FX options provide more tailored ways to manage currency exposure.

📊 Why FX matters even if you never trade it
Any international investment carries currency risk on top of the asset's own risk. A Czech investor buying US stocks is exposed to both the stock and the USD/CZK rate — the shares can rise while a falling dollar wipes out the gain in koruna terms. This is why global investors constantly decide whether to hedge their currency exposure, and FX instruments are how they do it.
⚠ What to watch
Currency risk is a separate layer. On any foreign asset, the exchange rate is a second bet stacked on the first. It can help or hurt independently of the asset.

FX is driven by macro, not fundamentals. Interest-rate differentials, central-bank policy, and capital flows move currencies — not earnings or dividends.

High leverage is standard. Retail FX is often traded with extreme leverage, which makes it one of the fastest ways to lose capital.

Hedging has a cost. Forwards remove uncertainty but at a price (the forward points); perfect certainty is never free.

Pairs are relative. You are never long a currency in isolation — always long one and short another.

Sources: HedgeStar — FX Hedging; Kotak Securities — Currency Market; Capital City Training — FX Derivatives.

Instrument 09 · Physical assets

Commodities

Physical goods — and why how you hold them matters as much as the price

Commodities are raw physical goods — gold, silver, oil, natural gas, agricultural products, industrial metals — traded on regulated commodity exchanges. They are the oldest form of trading, and they appeal to investors for two reasons: diversification (they often move differently from stocks and bonds) and as an inflation hedge (real assets tend to hold value when money loses it). But commodities hide a trap that catches many beginners: how you gain exposure can matter as much as the commodity's price itself.

Spot vs. futures exposure

You can get commodity exposure two fundamentally different ways. The spot approach means owning the physical thing (gold bars) or a fund that holds it — your return tracks the actual price closely. The futures approach means holding derivative contracts on the future price, which most commodity funds use because storing oil or wheat is impractical. The catch is that futures-based exposure must "roll" expiring contracts into new ones, and that rolling has a cost or benefit depending on the shape of the futures curve.

Contango — the hidden drag on futures-based funds

When longer-dated futures cost more than near-dated ones (a state called contango), a futures-based fund sells low and buys high every time it rolls — bleeding value even if the spot price is flat. The opposite (longer-dated cheaper) is backwardation, which helps. The effect is large: in some years a physically-backed gold fund can return many times what a broad futures-based commodity fund does, despite both being "commodity exposure." The structure of the product partly determines your return — a direct echo of the spot-vs-forward distinction from the first article.

How investors actually access commodities
Physically-backed ETF — holds the actual commodity (gold, silver). Tracks spot closely, no roll cost. Best for precious metals (e.g. GLD for gold, SLV for silver).
Futures-based ETF — holds futures contracts and rolls them (e.g. USO for oil, UNG for gas). Exposed to contango/backwardation — the roll can help or hurt.
Producer-equity ETF — holds shares of mining/energy companies. Behaves more like equity than the commodity itself (adds company and market risk).
Direct futures / physical — buying the contracts or the metal outright; involves margin, rolling, or storage and logistics.
⚠ What to watch
Structure can dominate price. With futures-based funds, contango can erode returns badly even when the commodity rises. Check how a fund gets its exposure.

Physically-backed tracks spot; futures-based may not. For a pure bet on gold, a physical fund avoids the roll problem.

Producer equities aren't the commodity. A gold-miner ETF carries company and equity-market risk on top of the gold price.

High volatility. Commodities swing hard on supply shocks, weather, and geopolitics — most guidance caps them at a small share of a portfolio (≈5–10%).

No income. Commodities pay no coupon or dividend; your entire return depends on price (and roll).

Conclusion · The whole map on one page

Bringing it together

Every instrument in this article is one of four things: a form of lending (money-market instruments, bonds, loans), a form of ownership (common and preferred shares), a blend (hybrids), or a contract deriving value from something else (derivatives, and the FX and commodity exposures built from them) — with funds as wrappers over any of these. The thread tying the claims together is the capital-structure ladder: the higher an instrument sits, the safer and lower-yielding; the lower, the riskier and higher-yielding. Master the ladder and the trade-off it encodes, and you can place any new instrument you meet — which is exactly the foundation the next article builds on to ask what each is actually worth.

Key takeaways

What to remember

  • Everything is lending, ownership, a blend, or a contract. Debt (money-market, bonds, loans), equity (common, preferred), hybrids (convertibles, mezzanine), derivatives — with funds as wrappers over any of them.
  • The capital-structure ladder is the key: senior secured debt → senior unsecured → subordinated → preferred → common. Higher = safer, lower-yielding; lower = riskier, higher-yielding.
  • Money-market = short & safe; bonds = a tradable loan whose price moves inversely to interest rates; loans = the most senior debt, often floating-rate and secured.
  • Common vs. preferred: votes + growth + residual (last) claim vs. no vote + fixed priority dividend + capped return. Preferred is a bond wearing an equity label.
  • Hybrids bridge the gap — convertibles are a bond plus an equity option; mezzanine is high-yield subordinated debt with equity kickers.
  • Funds are wrappers, not new claims: active vs. passive is the key split, ETFs trade intraday while mutual funds price once at NAV, and an ETN is a bank IOU — not a fund.
  • Derivatives derive value from an underlying: forwards/futures obligate both sides; options give the buyer a right (call = buy, put = sell) for a premium. Leverage magnifies both ways.
  • FX adds a separate currency-risk layer on any foreign asset; forwards are the main hedging tool. It's the largest market, driven by macro not fundamentals.
  • With commodities, structure can matter as much as price: futures-based funds suffer contango, physically-backed funds track spot. They pay no income and are volatile.

Glossary

Pojmy napříč všemi devíti skupinami nástrojů.

Capital-structure ladder / seniority
The strict order in which claims are paid if a company fails: senior secured debt → senior unsecured → subordinated debt → preferred equity → common equity. Higher = safer, lower-yielding.
Discount basis
Selling an instrument below face value and redeeming at full face value; the difference is the return (used for T-bills, commercial paper).
T-bill / Commercial paper / CD / Repo / Bankers' acceptance
Core money-market instruments: short-term government debt / unsecured short-term corporate debt / bank time deposit / short-term collateralised loan / bank-guaranteed trade IOU.
Face (par) value
The amount a bond repays at maturity; also the base for the coupon. Quoted on a base of 100.
Coupon
The annual interest a bond pays, as a percentage of face value.
Yield to maturity (YTM)
The total annualised return on a bond if held to maturity, accounting for price, coupons and time.
Duration
The sensitivity of a bond's price to a change in interest rates (covered in the valuation article).
Callable bond
A bond the issuer can repay early — usually when rates fall — carrying extra risk (and higher yield) for the investor.
STRIPS / FRN
A Treasury split into separately-traded zero-coupon pieces / a bond whose coupon floats with market rates.
Term loan / Revolver
A lump sum borrowed and repaid on schedule / a flexible credit line drawn and repaid as needed (the most senior debt).
First-lien / Second-lien
The ordering of claims on collateral: first-lien is paid first from the collateral (safest, lowest yield); second-lien ranks behind it.
Leveraged loan
A senior, secured, floating-rate loan to a below-investment-grade company; the safest slice of a risky borrower's debt.
Syndication
A lead bank underwrites a large loan then sells portions to other lenders and institutional investors, spreading the risk.
Covenant / Covenant-lite
Contractual rules protecting lenders (maintenance = tested continuously, incurrence = only on a specific action); cov-lite loans lack maintenance covenants.
Common stock
Full ownership: voting rights, a residual (last-in-line) claim, variable dividends, and unlimited upside. The highest-risk part of the structure.
Preferred stock
A debt-equity hybrid: usually no vote, a fixed priority dividend, paid before common (after all debt), with a capped return.
Residual claim
The equity holder's entitlement to whatever is left after all creditors are paid — unlimited upside, but last in line.
Cumulative preferred
Preferred whose missed dividends accumulate and must be paid in full before any common dividend.
Liquidation preference
In venture capital, the multiple (1x, 2x) a preferred investor gets back before common holders share the rest; participating preferred also shares in the remainder.
Convertible bond
A bond the holder can convert into a set number of common shares; pays a lower coupon in exchange for that upside option. Debt with an embedded equity option.
Mezzanine
Subordinated debt sitting between senior debt and equity, often with equity "kickers" (warrants); high yield, high risk, common in buyouts.
Hybrid security
An instrument blending debt and equity features (convertibles, preferred, mezzanine), sitting between the rungs of the capital-structure ladder.
Fund (wrapper)
A pooled vehicle that buys a basket of underlying instruments; its risk is the blended risk of its holdings, not a new claim type.
Active vs. passive
Active funds pick securities to beat the market (higher fees); passive (index) funds track an index cheaply. Most active managers lag after fees.
Mutual fund
A fund priced once daily at NAV, bought/sold with the fund company; often actively managed.
ETF (exchange-traded fund)
A fund that trades on an exchange all day like a stock, usually passive, low-fee, and transparent.
ETN (exchange-traded note)
A bank's debt obligation that tracks an index — not a basket of assets, so it carries the issuer's credit risk.
NAV (net asset value)
The per-share value of a fund's holdings; the price at which a mutual fund transacts once per day.
Derivative
A contract whose value is derived from an underlying asset or price; used to hedge, speculate, or arbitrage. Has no intrinsic value of its own.
Forward / Future
An agreement to transact at a set price on a future date; forward = bespoke/OTC (counterparty risk), future = standardised/exchange-traded/cleared. Both parties are obligated.
Call / Put option
The right (not obligation) to buy (call) or sell (put) at a strike price. Bought to bet on a rise (call) or fall (put), or to insure a holding.
Premium (option)
The price paid for an option — the buyer's maximum loss and the writer's maximum gain.
Strike price
The fixed price at which an option can be exercised.
Swap
An agreement to exchange streams of cash flows (e.g. fixed-for-floating interest). Mostly OTC; the backbone of the OTC derivatives market.
Leverage
Controlling a large exposure with a small outlay; magnifies both gains and losses.
Spot / Forward FX
Immediate currency exchange at the current rate / an agreement to exchange currency later at a rate locked in now (the main currency-hedging tool).
Currency risk
The exchange-rate exposure on any foreign asset — a second bet stacked on the asset's own return.
Contango / Backwardation
Futures curve shapes: contango (later contracts dearer) erodes futures-fund returns on each roll; backwardation (later cheaper) helps.
Physically-backed vs. futures-based
Two ways a commodity fund gets exposure: holding the actual asset (tracks spot) vs. holding and rolling futures (exposed to contango).
