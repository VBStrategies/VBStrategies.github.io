Europe's Capital Markets

Where European companies are listed and traded, why the market is fragmented, and what is changing

Earlier articles described markets in general. This one looks at the specific market my portfolio lives in: the exchanges, indices and infrastructure of continental Europe, the reasons it has fallen behind the US in size, and the reforms that aim to change that.

Objective

My portfolio thesis is about Europe: competitive, self-sufficient European companies that help the continent lead again. A thesis about Europe needs an honest picture of the market where those companies are actually traded — where they list, which indices measure them, how money flows through the system, and why Europe's equity market is smaller and cheaper than America's. Without that, "invest in Europe" is only a slogan.

So this article answers four questions: where European equities trade, how the European market is measured, why it is fragmented and under-sized, and what is being done about it — and what all of that means for my own portfolio.

Part 1 · The map

Where European equities trade

Exchange groups, alternative venues and post-trade plumbing

Continental Europe has no single stock exchange. It has several large exchange groups, each running national markets plus the clearing and settlement behind them:

Euronext — a pan-European group with regulated exchanges in Amsterdam, Brussels, Dublin, Lisbon, Milan, Oslo, Paris and, since it took a majority stake in the Athens exchange in November 2025, Athens. As of June 2026 it hosted over 1,800 issuers with about €7 trillion of market capitalisation.
Deutsche Börse — Frankfurt and its electronic platform Xetra, the derivatives exchange Eurex (home of Euro Stoxx 50 and Bund futures) and the settlement and custody house Clearstream.
SIX — the Swiss exchange in Zurich, where heavyweights such as Nestlé, Novartis and Roche are listed and trade in Swiss francs.
Nasdaq Nordic and BME — the Nordic and Baltic exchanges (Stockholm, Copenhagen, Helsinki) and the Spanish exchanges.
London (LSE) — historically Europe's largest venue, but outside the EU since Brexit and outside the scope of my portfolio.

Since MiFID I (2007) a share is no longer traded only on its home exchange. Multilateral trading facilities such as Cboe Europe and Aquis, plus systematic internalisers run by banks, compete for the same orders, so liquidity in one stock is spread across several venues.

Behind every trade sits post-trade infrastructure: central counterparties (CCPs) that guarantee trades and central securities depositories (CSDs) that settle them and record ownership. In the US this is largely concentrated in a single system; in Europe it is still split along national lines, and that split is a cost every investor pays, mostly without seeing it.

Index composition shapes what "Europe" means in practice. The continental market is concentrated: France, Germany and Switzerland made up roughly 55% of MSCI Europe ex UK as of March 2026. Sector weights also differ sharply from the US: Europe is heavy in financials, industrials, health care and consumer staples, and light in technology. A European portfolio is therefore not just a geographic bet but also a sector bet — something to keep in mind when comparing it with a global benchmark.

Europe vs. the US — size and price
From 2007 to mid-2025 the US share of advanced economies' stock-market capitalisation rose from about 42% to 62%, while Europe's share fell from about 34% to 21%. US stocks made up roughly 63% of the MSCI ACWI IMI in mid-2026. European equities also trade cheaper: around 15× forward earnings for MSCI Europe ex UK against about 22× for MSCI USA. In 2025, European equities outperformed the US by double digits for the first time since the financial crisis.
Part 3 · The structural problem

Why Europe is fragmented and under-sized

Banks, borders and savings habits

Europe's equity market is small relative to its economy for reasons that run deeper than any single year's performance:

Bank-based financing. European companies have traditionally borrowed from banks rather than raising money on capital markets, so fewer firms list and those that do list later.
National borders in law. Insolvency law, tax treatment, company law and supervision still differ country by country, which makes cross-border investing more complex and expensive.
Fragmented infrastructure. Many exchanges, CCPs and CSDs mean higher trading and settlement costs and thinner liquidity per venue.
Savings sitting in deposits. European households hold a large share of their wealth in bank deposits, and pension systems in many countries are pay-as-you-go rather than funded, so less domestic capital flows into equities.
Scale-up gap. Fast-growing European companies often turn to US investors — or list in New York — when they need large amounts of growth capital.

These are exactly the obstacles that the Letta and Draghi reports of 2024 identified as a drag on European competitiveness.

Part 4 · What is changing

From Capital Markets Union to Savings and Investments Union

The EU's attempt to build one market

The EU has tried to integrate its capital markets since the first Capital Markets Union action plan in 2015. In March 2025 the Commission relaunched the effort as the Savings and Investments Union (SIU), built on the idea of giving citizens better ways to grow their savings while making it easier for European businesses to raise the funding they need. Its strands cover citizens and savings, investment and financing, market integration and supervision.

The centrepiece so far is the Market Integration and Supervision Package, proposed in December 2025. It aims to remove barriers to cross-border activity, simplify rules and strengthen supervision at EU level, and includes changes to post-trade infrastructure. As of 2026 it is being negotiated in the Council and Parliament; whether national governments accept more centralised supervision is the key political question.

What to watch
Supervision. Does ESMA gain direct supervision of large cross-border players, or do national supervisors keep control?
Pensions and savings accounts. Funded pensions and simple savings accounts could bring a lasting flow of domestic money into European equities.
Listings and IPOs. Whether European scale-ups list at home rather than in New York.
Defence and industrial policy. Public spending programmes can shift earnings across sectors — a theme in its own right in my portfolio.
Part 5 · My portfolio

What this means for VB Europe Strategic

Turning the market picture into rules

Three practical consequences follow for my portfolio. First, the benchmark: because I exclude the UK, a broad index that includes it would be the wrong yardstick; an ex-UK European index is the honest comparison. Second, currency: Swiss holdings trade in francs, most others in euros, a few in Nordic currencies — so returns must be converted into my base currency, CHF. Third, expectations: Europe being cheaper than the US is not a reason to invest by itself. A valuation gap can persist for years; my thesis has to rest on companies and earnings, with structural reform as a possible tailwind rather than the core of the case.

Conclusion

Bringing it together

Continental Europe is a large but fragmented market: several exchange groups, competing trading venues and national post-trade systems, measured by indices that are concentrated in a few countries and tilted towards older-economy sectors. It has lost ground to the US in size and trades at a discount, for structural reasons the EU is now trying to address through the Savings and Investments Union. For an investor, the opportunity and the risk come from the same place: if integration succeeds, European companies gain access to deeper capital; if it stalls, the discount may simply stay.
