Regulation and Fund Structures

UCITS, AIFs and MiFID II — the rules that shape how money is managed in Europe, Liechtenstein and Switzerland

Asset management is one of the most regulated activities in finance. The rules decide what a fund may hold, who may buy it, how it must be sold, and what investors must be told. This article maps the main European frameworks and the position of Liechtenstein and Switzerland within them.

Context article; this is a general overview, not legal advice.

Objective

I want to work in asset and investment management in the DACH region — and I live in Liechtenstein, a fund centre that sits between the EU and Switzerland. The structures a fund can take and the rules it lives under are part of the daily work in this industry, and they are also a source of good discipline for my own portfolio: regulated funds have diversification limits, disclosure duties and conduct rules for very good reasons.

The article covers the two families of European funds (UCITS and AIFs), the rules for selling investments (MiFID II), and the special positions of Liechtenstein and Switzerland.

Part 1 · Retail funds

UCITS — the European standard

A fund passport built on strict limits

UCITS (Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities) is the EU framework for funds that can be sold to the general public. Once authorised in one member state, a UCITS can be marketed across the EU and EEA with a passport. The brand travels well beyond Europe, and the European fund industry is large: UCITS and alternative funds together held about €27.5 trillion at the end of the second quarter of 2026.

Eligible assets — mainly liquid, transferable securities (listed shares and bonds), money-market instruments, deposits, other funds and derivatives within limits.
Diversification — the 5/10/40 rule — no more than 10% of assets in one issuer, and holdings above 5% may together make up no more than 40% of the fund.
Liquidity — investors must be able to redeem regularly, at least twice a month and in practice usually daily.
Leverage — global exposure from derivatives is capped.
Investor information — a prospectus and a short, standardised key information document (KID).
Safekeeping — assets held by an independent depositary.
Part 2 · Alternative funds

AIFs and the AIFM Directive

Regulating the manager rather than the product

Everything that is not a UCITS is an alternative investment fund (AIF): private equity and venture funds, hedge funds, real-estate funds, infrastructure and private-credit funds. The AIFM Directive regulates mainly the manager — authorisation, capital, risk and liquidity management, valuation, depositary and reporting — rather than imposing product limits. AIFs are mostly sold to professional investors, and they can use leverage, illiquid assets and concentrated positions that a UCITS cannot.

Part 3 · Selling investments

MiFID II — conduct and markets

How investments may be sold and traded

The Markets in Financial Instruments Directive (MiFID II), in force since 2018, governs investment firms and trading venues. For investors, its core is client protection:

Client categories — retail clients, professional clients and eligible counterparties, with protection falling as experience rises.
Suitability and appropriateness — advice and portfolio management must fit the client's knowledge, experience, financial situation and objectives.
Product governance — manufacturers define a target market for each product.
Inducements and costs — limits on commissions and full disclosure of all costs.
Best execution — orders must be executed on the best terms reasonably available.

It works alongside the Market Abuse Regulation (insider dealing and market manipulation) and the PRIIPs rules on key information documents. Sustainability disclosures come from a separate regulation, SFDR — covered in the article on sustainable finance.

Part 4 · Where I live

Liechtenstein and Switzerland

Two neighbours, two regimes

Liechtenstein is in a customs and currency union with Switzerland — it uses the Swiss franc — but as a member of the European Economic Area it applies EU financial-market law, including UCITS, AIFMD and MiFID II, supervised by the Financial Market Authority (FMA). A fund set up in Vaduz can therefore use the EU passport. That bridge position is one of the foundations of Liechtenstein's fund and wealth-management industry.

Switzerland is not in the EEA and has its own regime: FINMA supervises, the Collective Investment Schemes Act (CISA) governs funds, and the Financial Services Act (FinSA) sets conduct rules similar in spirit to MiFID II. Swiss funds are not UCITS and have no EU passport; foreign funds offered to Swiss retail investors need FINMA approval.

Part 5 · My portfolio

What I take from this for VB Europe Strategic

Borrowing discipline from regulation

My portfolio is a personal project, not a regulated fund, so none of these rules apply to it legally. But they are tested answers to real risks, and I want to borrow from them. The statute plays the role of a prospectus: objective, universe, process and limits written down in advance. A UCITS-style diversification limit — no single position above 10%, and positions above 5% together capped at 40% — is a sensible default to test against the weights my statistical methods suggest. And reporting costs and risks as openly as a KID would is part of doing it properly.

Conclusion

Bringing it together

European asset management runs on two tracks: UCITS for liquid, diversified funds that can be sold to anyone and passported across Europe, and AIFs for everything alternative, with a regulated manager. MiFID II sets the rules for how investments are sold and traded. Liechtenstein combines the Swiss franc with EU financial law; Switzerland runs its own parallel system. For a student heading into this industry, these frameworks are the vocabulary of the job.
