Model · Intrinsic value

Adjusted present value (APV)

Values the business as if it had no debt, then adds the tax savings from debt and subtracts the expected cost of financial distress. The right model when the amount of debt is going to change.

Best forChanging leverage: LBOs, recapitalisations
Main inputsFCFF, unlevered beta, debt plan, default risk
EffortModerate: one extra step per debt effect
On this page The idea · Unlevered value · Tax shields · Distress costs · APV or WACC? · Worked example · When it misleads · Common mistakes · In the strategy · Sources

1 · The idea

The adjusted present value model, introduced by Stewart Myers in 1974, values a company in two steps. First, as if it had no debt at all. Then it adds what debt changes: the tax saved on interest and the expected cost of getting into financial trouble.

APV = Unlevered firm value + PV of interest tax shields − PV of expected distress costs

The WACC folds the effect of debt into the discount rate. APV keeps it separate and visible, which makes it the better tool whenever the amount of debt is going to change.

2 · Step 1: the unlevered firm

Unlevered value = Σ FCFFt ÷ (1 + ku)t + Terminal value ÷ (1 + ku)n  ·  ku = Risk-free rate + Unlevered beta × Equity risk premium

The cash flows are the same FCFF as in the FCFF model. Only the discount rate changes: the unlevered cost of equity, which reflects business risk alone. The unlevered beta comes from peers, exactly as on the inputs page.

3 · Step 2: the tax shields

Interest is tax-deductible, so each euro of interest saves the tax rate in taxes. The value of those savings depends on how risky they are, which depends on how debt will behave:

Debt policyPV of tax shieldsDiscount rateUsed in
Permanent, fixed debtTax rate × DebtCost of debtDamodaran’s standard APV; Modigliani and Miller
Debt grows with the firm (fixed debt ratio)Tax rate × Interest ÷ (ku − g)Unlevered cost of equityKoller et al.; the WACC also assumes this
Scheduled debt (repaid on a plan)Each year’s tax saving discountedCost of debtLeveraged buyouts, project finance

The tax shield only exists if the company has taxable profit to deduct the interest from. A company with losses gains nothing from debt.

4 · Step 3: expected distress costs

Expected distress cost = Probability of default × Cost of distress (as % of unlevered value) × Unlevered value

More debt raises the chance that the company cannot pay. Distress has direct costs (lawyers, advisers) and larger indirect ones: customers, suppliers and employees leave, and investment is cut. Andrade and Kaplan (1998) estimated the costs of financial distress at 10–20% of firm value in a sample of highly leveraged transactions. The probability of default can be read from the default rates of the credit rating the company would have at the new level of debt.

This term is what makes APV more than a tax calculation: without it, more debt would always look better.

5 · APV or WACC?

With consistent assumptions both give the same value. They differ in what is convenient:

SituationWACCAPV
Debt ratio stable over timeSimple and standardWorks, but no advantage
Debt changes a lot (LBO, recapitalisation, repayment plan)The WACC changes every year and must be recomputedNatural: tax shields follow the debt schedule
Question about the effect of debt itselfHidden in the discount rateVisible as a separate line

6 · Worked example

Nordwerk AG from the inputs page in stable growth: FCFF €175m growing at 3%, unlevered beta 0.90, risk-free rate 2.5%, equity risk premium 5.5%, tax 25%, debt including leases €780m, cash €180m, 100m shares.

The WACC route gave €35.90. The gap of about 1% comes from the tax shield assumption: treating debt as permanent gives €195m, while letting debt grow with the firm and discounting the shields at the unlevered rate gives €181m. The default probability of 2% is illustrative.

A leveraged recapitalisation

Suppose Nordwerk borrows another €800m and uses it to buy back shares at €36. Debt rises to €1,580m, interest cover falls from about 9.6x to about 3.5x (assuming the interest rate rises from 4% to 5.5%), and the default probability is assumed to rise to 8%.

The remaining shareholders gain about €2 per share, because the extra tax saving outweighs the extra expected distress cost at these assumptions. At a higher default probability or higher distress costs, the gain shrinks and turns negative; that trade-off is how Damodaran searches for a company’s optimal debt level. In practice the share price would react when the plan is announced, so part of the gain goes to the shareholders who sell in the buyback.

APV makes visible what the WACC hides: how much of the value comes from the business and how much from the way it is financed.

7 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenDebt will change substantially: leveraged buyouts, recapitalisations, companies paying down acquisition debt; valuing the effect of a financing decision.
Misleads whenDistress costs are ignored, so more debt always adds value; tax shields are counted although the company pays little tax; the default probability does not rise with debt.

8 · Common mistakes

  • Leaving out expected distress costs.
  • Discounting FCFF at the levered cost of equity or the WACC instead of the unlevered rate.
  • Valuing tax shields on debt the company will not actually have, or without enough taxable profit.
  • Mixing debt policies: permanent debt for the shields, but a debt ratio that grows with the firm elsewhere.
  • Treating the result of a recapitalisation as free money, without asking who captures it.

9 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic APV is used for companies whose leverage is about to change: holdings paying down acquisition debt, companies announcing large buybacks financed with debt, and businesses owned or targeted by private equity. It also links directly to the LBO analysis.
