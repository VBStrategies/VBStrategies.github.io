Model · Relative valuation

Trading comparables

Values a company by what the market pays for similar listed companies, scaled by a common measure such as EBITDA or earnings. It is the most widely used valuation method in practice, and the easiest to use badly.

Best forMature firms with many listed peers
Main inputsPeer prices, net debt, EBITDA, earnings, book value
EffortHours, not days
On this page The idea · Formula and the two families · Choosing peers · Building the multiples · Which multiple · What drives a multiple · Adjusting for differences · Worked example · When it misleads · Common mistakes · In the strategy · Sources

1 · The idea

Comparables rest on a simple principle: similar assets should sell for similar prices. If the market values a group of listed engineering companies at around ten times their EBITDA, a similar engineering company should be worth roughly the same multiple of its own EBITDA.

The method does not ask what a company is worth in absolute terms. It asks what it is worth relative to how the market prices its peers today. That is both its strength and its weakness. It is grounded in real prices that investors are paying, but it inherits whatever mood the market is in. If the whole sector is overpriced, comparables will tell you the target is fairly valued at an inflated price.

Practitioners therefore treat comparables as a market check on intrinsic value, not a replacement for it. Investment bankers routinely present them alongside a DCF, precedent transactions and, where relevant, an LBO analysis, and look for the range where the methods agree.

2 · The formula and the two families

Value of target = Peer multiple × Target's metric
for example: Enterprise value = median peer EV/EBITDA × target EBITDA

A multiple standardises price so that companies of different size can be compared. There are two families, and the numerator and denominator must always belong to the same one:

FamilyNumeratorPairs withExamples
Enterprise value multiplesEnterprise value: the claim of all capital providersMetrics before interest, belonging to debt and equity holders alikeEV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT, EV/Sales, EV/invested capital
Equity multiplesMarket capitalisation: the claim of shareholders onlyMetrics after interest, belonging to shareholders aloneP/E, P/B, price/cash flow, dividend yield

Mixing them breaks the logic. Enterprise value divided by net income compares the value of debt and equity with a profit that has already paid the debt holders. A highly indebted company would look cheap for no real reason.

Enterprise value multiples are usually preferred when peers have different levels of debt, because they look at the business before financing choices. Equity multiples are natural for banks and insurers, where debt is part of the operating business rather than a financing decision.

3 · Choosing peers

Peer selection matters more than any calculation that follows. A perfect formula applied to the wrong peers gives a precise wrong answer.

A good peer is similar in what actually determines value: how the company makes money, how fast it grows, how risky it is and how much it must reinvest. The usual order of criteria:

  1. Business model and end markets. Same products or services, sold to the same kinds of customers.
  2. Growth profile. A company growing at 15% a year should not be priced off peers growing at 3%.
  3. Profitability. Operating margins and returns on capital in a similar range.
  4. Risk. Similar cyclicality, customer concentration and, for equity multiples, similar leverage.
  5. Size and liquidity. Very small companies often trade at a discount for liquidity and higher risk.
  6. Geography and listing. Matters less than business model, but country risk, tax and accounting standards can differ.

How many? Typically five to ten. Fewer makes the median fragile; more usually means the definition of "similar" has been stretched. If only two or three true peers exist, it is better to say so and rely more on intrinsic value.

Where to find them: the competitors named in the target's annual report, the holdings of sector and thematic ETFs, industry classifications such as GICS, and screeners filtering by sector, region and size.

A useful test: if you cannot explain in one sentence why a company is a peer, it probably is not one.

4 · Building the multiples correctly

Most errors in comparables are not in the choice of multiple but in how the numbers behind it are assembled. Every peer must be measured the same way.

Enterprise value

EV = Market capitalisation (diluted) + Debt + Lease liabilities + Minority interests + Preferred shares − Cash and liquid investments − Non-operating assets
  • Diluted shares. Include options and convertibles that are in the money. The treasury stock method assumes option proceeds are used to buy back shares: additional shares = options × (1 − strike ÷ share price).
  • Leases. Under IFRS 16, lease liabilities sit on the balance sheet and lease costs are excluded from EBITDA. If EBITDA excludes lease costs, enterprise value must include lease liabilities, and the reverse.
  • Minority interests. If the company consolidates a subsidiary it does not fully own, EBITDA includes 100% of that subsidiary, so enterprise value must include the minority's share.
  • Associates and non-operating assets. Stakes accounted for by the equity method are not in EBITDA, so their value is deducted from enterprise value.

The metric

  • Same period. Either last twelve months (LTM) for all peers, or next year's forecast for all. Forward multiples are more common for investment decisions because price reflects expectations.
  • LTM calculation. LTM = last full year + current year-to-date − prior year same period.
  • Calendarisation. If a peer's financial year ends in March, convert to a calendar year: calendar year = (3/12) × FY ending March this year + (9/12) × FY ending March next year.
  • Clean the numbers. Remove one-off items such as restructuring charges, impairments or disposal gains, and do it the same way for every company.
  • Same currency. Convert price and metric at consistent rates, or use multiples, which are currency-neutral as long as numerator and denominator share a currency.

Using two or three multiples is common. When they point in the same direction, confidence rises. When they diverge, the divergence usually reveals something about debt, tax or margins that is worth understanding.

6 · What drives a multiple

Every multiple is a compressed discounted cash flow model. Rearranging a simple constant-growth model shows which fundamentals each multiple depends on. Damodaran calls the most important of these the "companion variable": the one fundamental a multiple is most sensitive to.

MultipleDerived from a constant-growth modelCompanion variable
P/E (forward)payout ratio ÷ (cost of equity − growth)Expected growth
P/B(ROE − growth) ÷ (cost of equity − growth)Return on equity
EV/Salesafter-tax operating margin × (1 − reinvestment rate) ÷ (WACC − growth)Operating margin
EV/EBITDARises with lower tax, lower reinvestment needs, lower WACC, higher growthReinvestment needs

This is the most useful idea on this page. A peer trading at a higher multiple is not "expensive" by default; it may simply have higher growth, higher returns or lower risk. A target only deserves the same multiple as its peers if it is similar on the companion variable. Comparables and DCF are therefore not rival methods but two views of the same economics.

7 · Adjusting for differences

Real peers are never identical. There are four ways to deal with the differences, from simplest to most rigorous:

  • Judgement within the range. Place the target in the upper or lower part of the peer range depending on whether its growth, margins or risk are better or worse than the median peer. Simple, but the argument must be written down.
  • Narrower peer group. Keep only the peers closest on the companion variable. Fewer peers, but more comparable ones.
  • Growth-adjusted multiples. The PEG ratio divides P/E by expected growth in percent. It corrects for growth crudely, assuming the relationship is linear, which it is not.
  • Sector regression. Regress the multiple on its drivers across all companies in the sector, for example EV/EBITDA on expected growth and margin. The regression gives a "fair" multiple for any combination of fundamentals, which can be applied to the target. This is Damodaran's preferred approach when enough companies are available.

One more adjustment concerns control. Trading comparables price small, minority stakes in companies. An acquirer buying the whole company usually pays a control premium, which is why precedent transactions typically show higher multiples than trading comparables.

8 · Worked example

A fictional industrial company, Nordwerk AG: EBITDA of €400m, net income of €220m, 100m diluted shares, debt of €700m, lease liabilities of €80m and cash of €180m, so net debt including leases is €600m. Expected growth is 6% a year, in line with peers.

Step 1: the peer table

Peer A8.5x13.0x14%Slower growth
Peer B9.2x14.5x16%
Peer C10.1x15.0x17%

Peer E11.5x17.5x21%Higher margins
Peer F14.0x24.0x19%Takeover speculation: outlier

The median ignores Peer F, whose price includes a possible takeover premium. The average would be pulled up by it. Nordwerk's own EBITDA margin is 17%, close to the median, so there is no obvious reason to place it far from the middle of the range.

Step 2: apply the multiples

Step 3: cross-check with P/E

15.5 × €220m net income = €3,410m equity value, or €34.10 per share. The two routes agree within 5%, which is reassuring. A large gap would point to a difference in debt, interest cost or tax between Nordwerk and its peers, since P/E is affected by all three and EV/EBITDA by none.

Step 4: test the sensitivity to peer choice

Dropping Peer F changes the median EV/EBITDA from 10.45x to 10.1x and the value per share to €34.40. Dropping Peer A instead moves it to 10.8x and €37.20. The result is reasonably robust, but the exercise shows how much a single peer can move the answer.

Result: a comparables range of roughly €32–39 per share, centred around €35. On a football field this becomes one bar, to be set against the DCF and other methods.

9 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenSeveral genuinely comparable listed peers exist; you need a market check on a DCF; you need a quick first estimate before building a full model; the market for the sector is liquid and well followed.
Misleads whenThe whole sector is over- or undervalued (you inherit the market's mistake); peers differ in growth, leverage or accounting; the target has no real peers; earnings are temporarily depressed or inflated.

10 · Common mistakes

  • Mixing enterprise value and equity measures in one multiple.
  • Comparing trailing multiples for some peers with forward multiples for others.
  • Using the average when one outlier dominates it.
  • Forgetting leases, minorities or associates in the enterprise value bridge.
  • Not cleaning one-off items, or cleaning them for the target but not for the peers.
  • Calling a company "cheap" because it trades below peers, without checking whether it has lower growth or higher risk.
  • Treating the result as a point estimate instead of a range.

11 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic, comparables are the standard cross-check for every company, never the only method:

  • Security and defence, industry and infrastructure, energy: EV/EBITDA against European peers, alongside a DCF.
  • Technology and chips: EV/EBIT or P/E, with care for very different growth rates; a sector regression on growth is often needed.
  • Health and life sciences: P/E and EV/EBITDA for established companies; drug pipelines need rNPV.
  • Private equity sleeve: price to net asset value against other listed private equity firms and holdings.
