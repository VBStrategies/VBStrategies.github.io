Real options

Values the flexibility to wait, expand or stop a project, using the tools of option pricing. It explains why some investments with a negative NPV today are still worth having.

Best forPatents, licences, staged projects
Main inputsProject value, investment, time, volatility
EffortModerate; volatility is the hard part
On this page The idea · Types · From finance to business · Worked example · When it misleads · Common mistakes · In the strategy · Sources

1 · The idea

A DCF assumes that a company decides today and then follows the plan whatever happens. In reality management can wait, expand when things go well, or stop when they go badly. That flexibility has value, because it lets the company capture the upside while limiting the downside, exactly like a financial option.

A real option is the right, but not the obligation, to take a business decision in the future. Real options valuation borrows the tools of option pricing to put a number on it.

2 · Types of real options

OptionThe rightLike aTypical case
Option to delayWait before investing until more is knownCallPatents, licences, undeveloped land or reserves
Option to expandInvest more if the first stage succeedsCallMarket entry, platform investments, new plants
Option to abandonStop and recover a salvage valuePutProjects with resaleable assets
StagingInvest in steps, each one a decisionCompound callDrug development, mining, R&D

3 · Translating the inputs

Financial optionReal option
Price of the underlying asset (S)Present value of the project’s cash flows
Strike price (K)Investment required to exercise
Time to expiry (T)How long the opportunity remains open: patent life, licence term, before a competitor moves
Volatility (σ)Uncertainty in the project value: from simulations, the volatility of comparable companies, or the variance of past project outcomes
Risk-free rate (r)Government bond yield for the period
Dividend yield (y)Cash flows lost by waiting; the cost of delay
Call value = S × e−yT × N(d1) − K × e−rT × N(d2)  ·  d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − y + σ²/2)T] ÷ σ√T,  d2 = d1 − σ√T

Black–Scholes gives a closed formula; a binomial tree, which lets the project value move up or down at each step, is more flexible and easier to explain, and handles early exercise.

4 · Worked example

Nordwerk AG can build a second defence electronics line, but only makes sense if European defence orders keep rising. Today the expected cash flows of the line are worth €500m and building it would cost €600m.

Static NPV = €500m − €600m = −€100m → reject

But Nordwerk does not have to decide today. It can wait up to three years, until order books are clearer, and build only if the line is then worth more than it costs. With volatility of the project value of 35%, a risk-free rate of 2.5% and no cash lost by waiting:

d1 = [ln(500/600) + (2.5% + 0.35²/2) × 3] ÷ (0.35 × √3)0.126
d2 = 0.126 − 0.35 × √3−0.480
N(d1), N(d2)0.550, 0.316
Option value = 500 × 0.550 − 600 × e−0.075 × 0.316€99m

A three-step binomial tree with the same inputs gives €103m. The project the DCF rejected is worth about €100m as an option, or about €1 per Nordwerk share. The value depends strongly on volatility: at 25% it is €65m, at 45% €133m.

If a competitor could take the market while Nordwerk waits, the cost of delay would be added as a dividend yield, and the option would be worth less. That is the main practical limit of the option to delay.

The lesson is not the exact number but the logic: an investment that looks negative today can be worth having the right to make, as long as the uncertainty is real and the right does not expire quickly.

5 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenThe company has a clear, exclusive right (a patent, licence, reserves, land); the decision really can wait; uncertainty will be resolved over time.
Misleads whenIt is used to justify any high price as "optionality"; the option is not exclusive; volatility is guessed; the option is already in the DCF forecast and is counted twice.

6 · Common mistakes

  • Calling every strategic hope a real option.
  • Using the volatility of the share price as the volatility of a single project.
  • Ignoring the cost of delay and competitors' ability to move first.
  • Adding option value to a DCF that already assumes the expansion happens.
  • Forgetting that the Black–Scholes assumptions (a traded underlying, continuous trading) hold only loosely for real assets.

7 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic real options are a cross-check, not a primary model. They are used for companies with exclusive rights to uncertain opportunities, such as drug patents, defence programmes that may be extended, or energy and resource licences, and to ask whether a gap between the DCF value and the market price could be explained by flexibility the DCF ignores.
