Model · Intrinsic value

Risk-adjusted NPV (rNPV)

Values a drug candidate by weighting each future cash flow with the probability that the drug survives to that point. The standard model for pharmaceutical and biotech pipelines.

Best forDrug pipelines, staged R&D projects
Main inputsPhase probabilities, timeline, peak sales
EffortModerate per asset
On this page The idea · Success probabilities · Building the model · rNPV and options · Worked example · When it misleads · Common mistakes · In the strategy · Sources

1 · The idea

A drug in clinical trials is a series of bets. Most candidates fail, and when one fails its future sales simply never happen. A standard NPV of the success case overvalues the drug enormously; raising the discount rate to compensate is crude and mixes two different kinds of risk.

The risk-adjusted NPV (rNPV) keeps them apart. Each future cash flow is multiplied by the probability that the drug will still be alive at that point, and then discounted at a normal cost of capital.

rNPV = Σ Probability of reaching year t × Cash flowt ÷ (1 + r)t

2 · The probabilities

The most widely cited source is the BIO, Informa Pharma Intelligence and QLS study of clinical development success rates for 2011–2020, covering 12,728 phase transitions:

TransitionSuccess rateChance of approval from this phase
Phase I → Phase II52.0%7.9%
Phase II → Phase III28.9%15.1%
Phase III → filing (NDA/BLA)57.8%52.4%
Filing → approval90.6%90.6%

Phase II is the hardest step: fewer than three in ten candidates pass it. A drug entering Phase II has about a 15% chance of reaching the market (28.9% × 57.8% × 90.6%). The study also shows large differences by disease area and drug type, so averages should be replaced by the relevant category when it is known.

3 · Building the model

  • Timeline: the remaining phases, their length and cost, filing and launch year.
  • Sales curve: patients, price and market share; a ramp-up over several years to peak sales, then a decline when the patent expires and generics or biosimilars enter.
  • Margins: production cost, sales and marketing, royalties to licensors, tax.
  • Probabilities: each cost is weighted by the chance of reaching the phase in which it is spent; each sales year by the overall chance of approval.
  • Discount rate: a normal cost of capital for an established company in the sector, because the clinical risk is already in the probabilities. Using a 30–40% venture capital rate on top double counts it.

A company with several candidates is valued as the sum of the rNPVs of its pipeline, plus marketed products at a normal DCF, minus central research and overhead costs. This is a sum of the parts in which every part has its own probability.

4 · rNPV and real options

rNPV treats each phase as a yes-or-no outcome, but management also has choices: stop the programme after weak Phase II data, expand into a second indication, license it out. Those choices are options, and they add value that rNPV ignores. The real options page shows how to value them; in practice rNPV is the standard, and options are used to argue about what it leaves out.

5 · Worked example

A fictional biotech, Helvion Bio, has one candidate entering Phase II. Assumptions: Phase II costs €30m a year for two years, Phase III €80m a year for three years, filing €10m in year 6, launch in year 7, peak sales €800m from year 11, patent expiry after year 16, operating margin 45%, tax 20%, cost of capital 9%.

Cash flows (present value)ProbabilityUnriskedRisked
Years 1–2: Phase II costs100%(€53m)(€53m)
Years 3–5: Phase III costs28.9%(€170m)(€49m)
Year 6: filing16.7%(€6m)(€1m)
Years 7–18: profits after tax15.1%€842m€127m
Total€613m€24m

The success case is worth €613m; the risk-adjusted value is only €24m. Almost the whole difference is the 85% chance that the drug fails. And the value is not stable: if Phase II succeeds, the value at the end of year 2 jumps to about €317m, because the chance of approval rises from 15% to 52%. If it fails, the asset is worth nothing.

This is why biotech shares move by large amounts on trial results. The rNPV is not the value the company will have; it is the average of two very different outcomes.

6 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenValuing drug candidates and pipelines; licensing deals; any project that passes through stages where it can fail.
Misleads whenIndustry averages are used for a candidate with clearly better or worse odds; peak sales are optimistic; the discount rate is raised on top of the probabilities.

7 · Common mistakes

  • Double counting risk: probabilities and a venture capital discount rate together.
  • Weighting costs with the overall probability of approval instead of the probability of reaching each phase.
  • Forgetting patent expiry and the fall in sales afterwards.
  • Using the all-disease average when the specific disease area has very different odds.
  • Presenting rNPV as the likely outcome, when the real outcome is either much higher or zero.

8 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic rNPV is used in the health and life sciences pillar, together with a DCF for marketed products. For large pharmaceutical groups the pipeline is valued as a sum of rNPVs and added to the value of the existing business; for small biotechs, where one trial can decide everything, position sizing matters as much as the valuation.
