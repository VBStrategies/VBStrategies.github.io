Model · Relative valuation

Precedent transactions

Values a company by the multiples acquirers paid for similar companies in past takeovers. Because buyers pay for control, it usually gives a higher value than trading comparables, and the difference tells you what a takeover might be worth.

Best forTakeover candidates, consolidating sectors, M&A pricing
Main inputsDeal prices, target financials at announcement
EffortData-hungry: deal details are hard to find

1 · The idea

Trading comparables ask what investors pay today for a small stake in similar companies. Precedent transactions ask a different question: what have buyers actually paid to own similar companies outright? The method uses the same multiples, EV/EBITDA, EV/Sales or P/E, but calculated at the price of completed acquisitions.

Value of target = Median transaction multiple × Target's metric

It is a standard part of every investment banking valuation, next to trading comparables, a DCF and an LBO analysis. For an investor in listed shares, its main use is to estimate what the company might be worth to an acquirer, and therefore how much upside a takeover could bring.

2 · Why takeovers cost more: the control premium

Acquirers almost always pay more than the share price before the bid. The difference is the control premium, and it has three sources:

  • Control itself. The owner decides strategy, management, dividends and capital structure. A minority shareholder decides none of these.
  • Synergies. Cost savings and extra revenue from combining two businesses. Buyers often share part of them with the seller to win the deal.
  • Competition. When several bidders want the same company, the price rises.

Premiums vary widely with the market and the deal. Large studies of public takeovers put the typical premium at around 30% over the undisturbed share price; for example, the FactSet Mergerstat/BVR Control Premium Study reports about 30% for the UK (BVR). This is why transaction multiples are not directly comparable with trading multiples: they answer a question about control, not about a minority stake.

3 · Choosing deals

The same logic as choosing peers for comparables applies, with two extra dimensions: time and type of buyer.

  1. Similar target. Same business model, end markets and, as far as possible, size and profitability.
  2. Recent. Usually the last five years or so. Older deals were struck in different interest rate and credit conditions; a deal from a peak year can mislead in a downturn and the reverse.
  3. Type of buyer. Strategic buyers (competitors) can count on synergies and often pay more; financial buyers (private equity funds) pay what their return targets and debt capacity allow.
  4. Type of deal. Full takeovers, not minority stakes; and note whether the price was paid in cash or shares, since share deals made at high valuations can inflate multiples.
  5. Distressed sales. Usually excluded, or treated separately: a forced seller accepts a lower price.

Finding even five good deals can be difficult. A small, carefully chosen set is better than a long list of loosely related transactions.

4 · Computing transaction multiples

Transaction EV = Offer price per share × Diluted shares + Net debt assumed + Minorities − Non-operating assets
  • Price: the final offer price, including any increases during the bidding process.
  • Metric: the target's last-twelve-months (LTM) figures at announcement, since that is what the buyer knew. Forward multiples are used when reliable forecasts at the time are available.
  • Debt: the net debt the buyer took on, from the target's last balance sheet before the deal.
  • Premium: offer price divided by the undisturbed share price, typically measured one day and four weeks before the announcement or before rumours started.

5 · Where to find the data

Professional databases such as Mergermarket, Capital IQ or Refinitiv collect deal multiples, but they are expensive. Without them:

  • Offer documents and announcements: listed targets must publish the offer terms, and the buyer's announcement often states the multiple paid.
  • Fairness opinions: in many public takeovers, the advisers' fairness opinion lists the comparable transactions they used.
  • Annual reports of serial acquirers: note disclosures on business combinations give the price and the target's revenue or profit.
  • Financial press and sector reports: often quote the EV/EBITDA paid in large deals.
  • University library: check whether students have access to a database.

The median transaction value of €43.60 is 22% above the trading comparables median of €35.80. That gap is the implied control premium, below the 31% median premium in the deals. A reasonable reading: an acquirer could justify paying around €41–46 per share, but only if it can realise synergies similar to the buyers in Deals 2–4.

For an investor, this is not the value of a share today; it is the value in a takeover scenario. It belongs on the football field as a separate bar, and its probability matters as much as its level.

7 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenValuing a company for a takeover; the sector is consolidating and the target is a plausible candidate; checking whether a bid is fair.
Misleads whenDeals are old or few; market conditions have changed; prices include synergies specific to one buyer; it is used to value a minority stake without adjustment.

8 · Common mistakes

  • Applying transaction multiples to value a minority investment, which adds a control premium the investor will never receive.
  • Mixing deals from very different interest rate environments without comment.
  • Using forward metrics for some deals and LTM for others.
  • Ignoring whether the price was paid in cash or in overvalued shares.
  • Keeping a single bidding-war deal that dominates a small sample.
  • Treating the result as certain rather than as a scenario with a probability.

9 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic the portfolio holds minority stakes, so transactions are never the primary valuation. They are used to:

  • estimate the upside if a holding becomes a takeover target, relevant in sectors that are consolidating, such as European defence;
  • value the private equity sleeve, where private equity firms realise their investments through sales to strategic or financial buyers;
  • check whether a bid for a holding is fair before deciding to accept or hold.
