Model · Intrinsic value

Net asset value (NAV)

Values a company as the sum of what its assets are worth, less everything that is owed or leaks away. The standard model for holdings, listed private equity and real estate companies.

Best forHoldings, listed private equity, real estate
Main inputsAsset values, debt, holding costs, leakage
EffortLow to high: depends on private holdings
On this page The idea · Valuing the assets · Versions · Holding costs and leakage · Discount to NAV · Worked example · When it misleads · Common mistakes · In the strategy · Sources

1 · The idea

For some companies the business is a portfolio of assets: an investment holding, a listed private equity firm, a real estate company. Their value is best measured by adding up what the assets are worth and subtracting what is owed. The result is the net asset value, NAV.

NAV = Market or fair value of assets − Debt − Other liabilities − Capitalised holding costs − Tax and carried interest leakage

The share price is then compared with NAV per share. Most holdings and listed private equity firms trade at a discount to NAV; a few trade at a premium.

3 · The versions of NAV

  • Reported NAV: what the company publishes. For private equity firms, the sum of the fund or portfolio marks.
  • Adjusted NAV: the analyst's own version, re-marking unlisted holdings with current peer multiples and deducting costs the reported figure leaves out.
  • Real estate (EPRA): since 2020 the European Public Real Estate Association recommends three measures: Net Reinstatement Value (NRV, the cost of rebuilding the company), Net Tangible Assets (NTA, the going concern view, the most widely used) and Net Disposal Value (NDV, as if the assets were sold, after taxes and with debt at fair value).
  • Look-through NAV: for holdings of holdings, value each layer through to the operating assets.

4 · Holding costs, tax and carried interest

Three items separate the value of the assets from what shareholders actually own:

  • Holding costs. Management, staff and listing costs continue for as long as the company exists. Capitalised as a perpetuity, annual costs of 0.5–1% of assets can be worth a large share of NAV.
  • Taxes on sale. If assets were sold, capital gains tax may be due. Whether to deduct it depends on whether a sale is realistic.
  • Carried interest and fees. In listed private equity, a share of the gains goes to managers, not shareholders.

5 · The discount or premium to NAV

Discount to NAV = 1 − Share price ÷ NAV per share

A discount is the market's way of deducting what the reported NAV leaves out or overstates. The usual reasons: holding costs, doubts about private marks, poor capital allocation, illiquidity, a controlling shareholder and lack of catalysts. A premium appears when investors expect the manager to keep creating value beyond the current assets. As with the conglomerate discount, a gap closes only with a catalyst: buybacks below NAV, asset sales that prove the marks, simplification or a wind-down.

Without holding costs and carried interest, a quick NAV would be €3,100m or €62 per share, and the share would look 29% cheap. After them, the discount is 20%. If the private marks are 15% too high, a realistic risk when listed peers have fallen and the marks have not followed yet, the discount shrinks to 10%. The headline discount was mostly explained.

The first question about a discount to NAV is not "is it cheap?" but "which part of the NAV is the market not believing, and is it right?"

7 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenThe company's value lies in identifiable assets: holdings, listed private equity, real estate, closed-end funds, companies in run-off or liquidation.
Misleads whenValue comes from future activity, such as new funds a private equity manager will raise; private marks are stale; costs and leakage are left out.

8 · Common mistakes

  • Ignoring holding costs, which are permanent.
  • Accepting private marks without comparing them with current listed peer multiples.
  • Treating every discount as an opportunity, without a catalyst.
  • Valuing a private equity manager (which earns fees) the same way as a private equity fund (which owns assets).
  • Double counting: adding a listed stake at market value and also its dividends in a cash flow model.

9 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic NAV is the primary model for the private equity sleeve and for investment holdings. Every NAV is adjusted for holding costs and carried interest, and private marks are tested against listed peers before a discount is called an opportunity.
