Leveraged buyout (LBO)

Estimates the highest price a private equity buyer could pay and still earn its target return. Not a measure of intrinsic value, but often a floor under the share price.

Best forStable cash generators, PE targets
Main inputsEntry price, debt, cash flows, exit multiple
EffortModerate: circular debt schedule
On this page The idea · How it works · Structure · Building the model · Worked example · When it misleads · Common mistakes · In the strategy · Sources

1 · The idea

A leveraged buyout is the purchase of a company by a financial sponsor, typically a private equity fund, financed largely with debt that the company itself will repay from its cash flows. An LBO analysis asks one question: what is the highest price a sponsor could pay and still earn its target return?

That makes it a different kind of valuation. It does not estimate what the company is worth to a long-term owner, but what one specific type of buyer could afford. Because private equity funds are active, well-financed buyers, that number often acts as a floor under the share price of companies that fit their profile.

2 · How an LBO creates returns

Sponsor equity = Purchase price + Fees − Debt raised  ·  Return = Equity at exit ÷ Equity at entry

The sponsor earns its return from three sources, which every LBO model should separate:

  • Growth in EBITDA: from revenue growth and better margins.
  • Debt paydown: cash flow repays debt, so a larger share of the enterprise value belongs to the equity at exit.
  • Multiple change: selling at a higher EV/EBITDA than the purchase multiple. This is the least controllable source; a prudent model assumes the exit multiple is no higher than the entry multiple.

Two return measures are used: the multiple of invested capital (MOIC, equity out ÷ equity in) and the internal rate of return (IRR), which also accounts for time.

How much debt a company can carry depends on how stable its cash flows are, its interest cover and the state of the credit market. The amount of debt is the main reason LBO values move with credit conditions: cheaper and more available debt lets sponsors pay more.

4 · Building the model, step by step

  1. Entry: purchase price (EV/EBITDA × EBITDA), fees, debt raised, sponsor equity.
  2. Projection: EBITDA, interest, tax, capital expenditure, working capital, usually for five years.
  3. Debt schedule: cash after interest and tax repays debt (a "cash sweep"); interest is computed on the outstanding balance.
  4. Exit: exit multiple × final EBITDA − remaining debt = equity at exit.
  5. Returns: MOIC and IRR; then solve backwards for the entry price that gives the target IRR.

With the same debt and exit assumptions, a sponsor targeting 15–20% could pay about €28–31 per share, below the intrinsic and trading values of €33–36. For Nordwerk, the LBO is therefore not a likely exit but a floor: if the share fell well below €28, the company would start to look interesting to private equity.

The LBO value depends heavily on credit conditions. When debt is cheap and plentiful, the floor rises; when credit tightens, it falls, regardless of the company itself.

6 · When to use it, and when it misleads

Use whenThe company has stable cash flows, low debt and could be a private equity target; estimating a valuation floor; judging whether a takeover bid from a sponsor is plausible.
Misleads whenThe company has volatile cash flows or high capital needs, so it could not carry much debt; credit markets are closed; it is read as the intrinsic value.

7 · Common mistakes

  • Assuming an exit multiple above the entry multiple without a reason.
  • Ignoring fees and the cost of debt arrangement.
  • Forgetting that interest depends on the debt balance, which depends on the cash flow: the model is circular and must be solved.
  • Leverage that leaves interest cover too thin to survive a downturn.
  • Treating the LBO price as what the company is worth, rather than what one buyer could pay.

8 · Where it fits in the strategy

In VB Europe Strategic the LBO analysis has two uses. It sets a floor for holdings with stable cash flows and low debt, and it is the key to understanding the private equity sleeve: listed private equity firms earn their returns from exactly these three sources, so their results can be read through this model.
