1 · From valuations to weights

Valuation tells you which companies are attractive. It does not tell you how much of each to hold. A portfolio of the five most undervalued shares, bought in equal amounts, can still be badly built: they may all depend on the same risk, or the most attractive one may also be the most uncertain.

Portfolio construction turns valuations into weights. It needs three inputs for every holding or pillar: an expected return (from the gap between price and fair value, as on the football field), a risk (volatility) and how holdings move together (correlations). This page follows the path from the classic Markowitz model to the Black–Litterman model that is used in practice.

2 · Mean-variance optimisation (Markowitz)

Harry Markowitz showed in 1952 that what matters is not the risk of each holding on its own but its contribution to the risk of the whole portfolio. Holdings that do not move together reduce risk without reducing return: diversification.

Portfolio return = Σ wi × μi  ·  Portfolio variance = Σ Σ wi × wj × σij = w' Σ w
  • Efficient frontier: the portfolios with the highest expected return for each level of risk.
  • Minimum-variance portfolio: the least risky combination, w = Σ−11 ÷ (1' Σ−11). It needs no expected returns at all.
  • Tangency portfolio: the highest return per unit of risk (Sharpe ratio), w ∝ Σ−1(μ − rf).

3 · Why raw Markowitz breaks in practice

The optimiser treats its inputs as exact. They are not: expected returns in particular are estimated with large errors. Michaud (1989) called mean-variance optimisation an "error maximiser", because it puts the most weight on the assets whose expected returns are most overestimated. The symptoms are extreme weights, large short positions and portfolios that change completely when one input moves slightly. The worked example below shows all three.

There are three standard repairs, used together: constraints on the weights, a better covariance estimate, and better expected returns through Black–Litterman.

4 · Repair 1: constraints

Constraints rule out the solutions that no investor would accept: no short sales, minimum and maximum weights per holding, sector or country limits. They cost a little expected Sharpe ratio on paper, and in exchange make the portfolio robust to estimation error. Benninga shows how to compute efficient portfolios without short sales, which can no longer be done with a formula and needs a numerical solver.

5 · Repair 2: a better covariance matrix

The sample covariance matrix from historical returns is noisy when the number of assets is large relative to the number of observations. With 40 shares and five years of monthly data, there are 820 variances and covariances to estimate from 60 observations each. The result is unstable, and the optimiser exploits its errors.

Shrunk covariance = δ × Target + (1 − δ) × Sample covariance

Ledoit and Wolf (2004) proposed shrinking the sample matrix towards a simple, structured target, such as a matrix in which all pairs have the same correlation, with the intensity δ chosen to minimise the expected estimation error. Their estimator is now standard and is available in most statistical software, for example in R and in Python's scikit-learn.

6 · Repair 3: Black–Litterman

Black and Litterman (1992) solved the problem of expected returns by not starting from them. They start from the market: if the benchmark is held by investors in equilibrium, its weights imply a set of expected returns. The investor's own views are then blended in, each with a stated confidence.

Implied equilibrium returns: Π = δ × Σ × wmarket
Combined returns: μBL = [(τΣ)−1 + P'Ω−1P]−1 × [(τΣ)−1Π + P'Ω−1Q]
  • δ is the market's risk aversion, τ scales the uncertainty of the equilibrium, both small and often set by convention.
  • P says which assets a view is about, Q what the view is (for example "defence will beat industry by 3% a year"), and Ω how uncertain it is.
  • Without views, the model returns the benchmark weights. With views, it tilts only the assets the views concern, and only as far as the confidence justifies.

This is why the model suits a valuation-driven investor: the valuations become the views, and the confidence in each view comes from how wide and how consistent its football field is. Benninga's chapter on Black–Litterman works through the model in a spreadsheet.

What the columns show

  • Markowitz shorts industry by 20% and puts 45% in health, the least volatile pillar. If the expected return of technology is raised by just one percentage point, its weight jumps from 18% to 26% and the industry short grows to 26%. That instability is the "error maximiser" at work.
  • Long-only removes the short and sets industry to zero. With a 10% minimum for every pillar, as the statute requires, the portfolio becomes reasonable at a cost of only 0.01 in Sharpe ratio on paper.
  • Black–Litterman starts from benchmark weights (20/30/15/20/15) and adds one view: security and defence will beat industry by 3% a year, held with the same confidence as the equilibrium. The implied return gap between the two widens from 0.3% to 1.6%, and only those two weights move: defence up from 20% to 34.7%, industry down from 30% to 15.3%. The other pillars stay at their benchmark weights, because there is no view on them.

Does shrinkage help?

In a simulation, 60 months of returns were drawn 1,000 times from the true covariance matrix and the minimum-variance portfolio was estimated each time. Portfolios built on the sample matrix had on average 3.6% more risk than the true optimum, those built on the Ledoit–Wolf matrix 3.0%. With only five pillars the gain is small; it grows with the number of assets, which is exactly the case Ledoit and Wolf designed it for.

The practical sequence: valuations become views, Black–Litterman turns views into expected returns, a shrunk covariance matrix measures risk, and constraints keep the result sensible.

8 · Checking the result: risk contributions

Risk contribution of holding i = wi × (Σw)i ÷ Portfolio variance

Weights do not show where the risk comes from. A pillar with 20% of the capital can carry 35% of the risk if it is volatile and correlated with the rest. The risk contributions add up to 100% and are the last check before a portfolio is accepted: no single pillar should dominate the risk unless that is a deliberate decision.

9 · Common mistakes

  • Feeding historical average returns into an optimiser as expected returns.
  • Trusting an unconstrained optimiser's weights.
  • Estimating a large covariance matrix from a short history without shrinkage.
  • Stating views in Black–Litterman with more confidence than the valuation supports.
  • Rebalancing to the optimiser's output every month, so that trading costs eat the expected gain.
