Valuation & Portfolio Toolkit

Every main way to value a company, when each one fits, and how to turn many valuations into one portfolio. One page per model, all built on the same template so they can be compared.

Three ways to value a company

RelativeWhat does the market pay for similar companies? Multiples such as EV/EBITDA and P/E.
IntrinsicWhat are the company's future cash flows worth today? DCF and its relatives.
Option-basedWhat is flexibility worth? Real options for pipelines and staged projects.

The three approaches can give very different answers. That is not a flaw: each one asks a different question, and the gap between them is information.

Where this table comes from

The table is a synthesis, not one author's rule. The main valuation references agree on the logic but weigh the methods differently:

  • Damodaran picks the model by asking what is measurable and stable: are cash flows positive and predictable, is the firm's debt changing, are its assets better valued as separate parts, and where is it in its life cycle?
  • The CFA Institute curriculum (Pinto et al.) gives explicit conditions: dividend discount models when dividends track profitability; free cash flow models when dividends differ from cash flow or a controlling stake is valued; residual income when there are no dividends, free cash flow is negative or unpredictable, and accounting is transparent.
  • McKinsey (Koller, Goedhart, Wessels) treats the enterprise DCF, or its equivalent economic profit model, as the core method for almost every company, with multiples as a check rather than a separate answer.
  • Investment banking practice (Rosenbaum and Pearl) uses four standard methods side by side: comparable companies, precedent transactions, DCF and LBO analysis, shown together on a football field.

Where they differ is mostly in emphasis. Academic and consulting texts start from intrinsic value; banking practice gives the market-based methods more weight because deals are priced against them. This toolkit follows the intrinsic-first view for investment decisions and uses the market-based methods as a test.

Other ways to choose a model

LensThe questionWhat it points to
Life cycleIs the firm young, growing, mature or declining?Young: DCF from target margins, EV/Sales · Growth: DCF, PEG · Mature: DCF, EV/EBITDA, DDM · Decline: liquidation value, NAV
PurposeMinority investment, takeover, or a lender's view?Minority: trading comparables, DDM · Takeover: transactions, FCFF/APV, LBO · Lender: downside cases, asset values
Data availableAre there reliable forecasts, listed peers, market prices for the assets?Good forecasts: DCF · Many peers: comparables · Marked assets: NAV
What creates valueCash flows, the balance sheet, or options on the future?Cash flows: DCF family · Balance sheet: residual income, NAV · Options: real options, rNPV

What to watch out for in each model

ModelThe trap
ComparablesInherits the market's mispricing; peers that are not really similar
Precedent transactionsOld deals in different markets; control premiums and synergies built into the price
DCF (FCFF, FCFE)Terminal value often 60–80% of the result; small changes in WACC or growth move it a lot
Dividend discountUseless when dividends do not reflect earning power, for example with large buybacks
APVRequires estimating tax shields and distress costs separately, both uncertain
Residual incomeOnly as good as the accounting; aggressive book values flatter the result
Sum of the partsIgnores costs and synergies at group level; the "conglomerate discount"
NAVAsset marks can be stale or optimistic, especially for private holdings
rNPVSuccess probabilities per trial phase are averages that may not fit the specific drug
LBOGives what a financial buyer could pay, not what the company is worth to a long-term owner
Real optionsVolatility and exercise assumptions are hard to estimate; easy to overvalue "potential"

The common thread: no single model is reliable alone. Each page in the library explains its model's weak points in detail, and the football field brings the results together.
