Measuring Portfolio Performance

Returns, risk, risk-adjusted ratios, benchmarks — and how to tell skill from luck

Running a portfolio means being judged by numbers. This article sets out how returns and risk are measured, which ratios compare portfolios fairly, and why a short track record proves much less than it seems.

Methodology article for VB Europe Strategic reporting. Depth: roughly CFA Level I (Quantitative Methods, Portfolio Management).

Objective

Once my portfolio is running, every report will show a return. The question is what that number actually says. A return without risk, without a benchmark, and without the right method of calculation can make an average strategy look brilliant — or a good one look poor. I want the measurement rules fixed before the results arrive, so I cannot choose the flattering method afterwards.

This article is therefore my methodology: how to calculate returns, how to measure risk, which risk-adjusted ratios to use, how to choose a benchmark, and how to stay honest about what a short record can and cannot prove.

Part 1 · Returns

Measuring return correctly

Holding-period, arithmetic vs. geometric, time- vs. money-weighted

The holding-period return is the basic building block: (ending value − beginning value + income) ÷ beginning value. Over several periods, two averages answer different questions. The arithmetic mean is the best estimate of a single future period's return; the geometric mean is the actual compound growth rate achieved. With volatile returns the geometric mean is always lower — a −50% year followed by +50% leaves you 25% down, though the arithmetic average is zero.

When money flows in or out of a portfolio, a second choice appears. The time-weighted return (TWR) chains the returns of each sub-period and removes the effect of the timing of deposits and withdrawals — it measures the manager. The money-weighted return (MWR) is the internal rate of return on all cash flows — it measures the investor's actual experience, including the luck or skill of when money was added. Industry standards (GIPS) require TWR for judging managers.

Worked example — same portfolio, two answers
Start with 100. Year 1: +20% → 120. The investor adds 100 → 220. Year 2: −10% → 198.
TWR: (1.20 × 0.90)1/2 − 1 = +3.9% a year — the strategy made money.
MWR: the IRR of −100, −100, +198 = −0.7% a year — the investor lost money, because more capital was in the portfolio during the bad year.
Part 2 · Risk

Measuring risk

Volatility, downside and drawdown
Standard deviation (volatility) — the dispersion of returns around their mean, usually annualised (monthly σ × √12). The standard measure of total risk.
Downside deviation — counts only returns below a target. Investors fear losses more than upside surprises.
Maximum drawdown — the largest fall from a peak to a subsequent trough. A path of 100 → 120 → 90 → 130 has a maximum drawdown of −25% (from 120 to 90), even though it ends higher.
Beta — sensitivity to the benchmark; the systematic part of risk.
Tracking error — the volatility of the difference between portfolio and benchmark returns; how far the portfolio deviates from the index.
Value at Risk (VaR) — the loss not expected to be exceeded with a given probability over a given horizon (e.g. 95%, one month). Useful, but silent about how bad the remaining 5% can be.
Part 3 · Risk-adjusted performance

Comparing portfolios fairly

Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen's alpha, Sortino, information ratio
MeasureFormulaWhat it answers
Sharpe ratio(Rp − Rf) / σpExcess return per unit of total risk
Treynor ratio(Rp − Rf) / βpExcess return per unit of systematic risk
Jensen's alphaRp − [Rf + βp(Rm − Rf)]Return above what CAPM predicts for the beta taken
Sortino ratio(Rp − target) / downside deviationExcess return per unit of downside risk
Information ratio(Rp − Rb) / tracking errorActive return per unit of active risk
Worked example
Portfolio A returns 9% with volatility 14%; portfolio B returns 7% with volatility 8%; risk-free rate 2%.
Sharpe A = (9 − 2) / 14 = 0.50; Sharpe B = (7 − 2) / 8 = 0.63.
A earned more, but B earned more per unit of risk. Levered to A's volatility, B would have returned more.

Use the Sharpe ratio when the portfolio is the investor's whole wealth; Treynor and alpha when it is one piece of a diversified whole; the information ratio when the task is to beat a specific benchmark.

Part 4 · Benchmarks and attribution

Compared with what?

Choosing a benchmark and explaining the difference

A good benchmark is specified in advance, investable, measurable and appropriate to the strategy — covering the same universe the manager actually chooses from. Changing the benchmark after the fact is one of the oldest ways of flattering results.

Performance attribution explains where the difference from the benchmark came from. The classic Brinson approach splits it into an allocation effect (over- or under-weighting sectors or countries that did well or badly) and a selection effect (picking better or worse stocks within them). For an investor whose base currency differs from the assets', a currency effect comes on top.

Part 5 · Skill or luck?

What a track record can prove

Statistics, biases and honesty

Returns are noisy, so even a skilled manager has bad years and an unskilled one good years. A rough test of whether active returns are statistically different from zero is the t-statistic ≈ information ratio × √(number of years).

The uncomfortable arithmetic
An information ratio of 0.5 is considered good. To reach a t-statistic of 2 (roughly 95% confidence), it needs (2 / 0.5)² = 16 years of data. One or two years of outperformance prove almost nothing.
Biases that inflate results
Survivorship bias. Databases that drop failed funds or delisted companies overstate average returns.
Look-ahead bias. Using information in a backtest that was not available at the time.
Backfill bias. Adding a fund's history only after it has done well.
Data snooping. Testing many strategies and reporting the one that worked.
Benchmark shopping. Picking the comparison that makes results look best.
Part 6 · My portfolio

The rules I want to apply to VB Europe Strategic

Fixed in advance

For my own reporting this translates into a short list: returns time-weighted and in CHF, including dividends; the benchmark fixed in the statute before the first trade; volatility, maximum drawdown, Sharpe ratio, tracking error and information ratio reported every month; currency effect shown separately; and an honest note that a track record of a year or two is a learning exercise, not proof of skill. The point of measuring is to learn what worked and why — not to produce a good-looking number.

Conclusion

Bringing it together

Performance measurement is a discipline of choosing the right question before computing the answer. Time-weighted returns judge the strategy, money-weighted returns the investor's experience. Risk has several faces — volatility, downside, drawdown, beta, tracking error — and risk-adjusted ratios make portfolios comparable. A benchmark has to be fixed in advance, attribution explains the gap, and statistics remind us how long it takes before any of this proves skill.
