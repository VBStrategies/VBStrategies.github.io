Sustainable Finance and Defence

What ESG investing is, how EU rules define it, and why defence has moved from the exclusion list into the debate

For years, defence companies were among the first names cut from "responsible" portfolios. Since 2022 that view has been challenged across Europe — by governments, regulators and investors. This article explains the framework, sets out the arguments on both sides, and states where my own portfolio stands.

Objective

My portfolio includes defence without exclusions inside the sector. That is a deliberate choice, and it runs against a decade of practice in European sustainable investing. A choice like that should be argued, not just stated — and it should be argued knowing the strongest case against it.

This article covers what ESG investing is, how the EU regulates it through SFDR, how the treatment of defence has changed, the arguments on each side, and the legal limits that apply regardless of one's view.

Part 1 · The framework

What ESG investing is

Several different things under one label

ESG stands for environmental, social and governance factors. The label covers approaches that are quite different from each other:

ESG integration — using ESG factors as financial risk inputs, like any other analysis (e.g. carbon costs, governance failures).
Exclusions (negative screening) — removing whole sectors or companies, such as tobacco, coal or weapons.
Best-in-class — holding the companies that score best within each sector.
Thematic and impact investing — targeting specific outcomes, such as renewable energy, with measurable impact.
Stewardship — voting and engagement to change company behaviour from the inside.

The distinction matters: integration asks whether ESG risks affect value; exclusion asks whether an activity is acceptable at all. Defence has mostly been handled by exclusion.

Part 2 · EU rules

SFDR and its overhaul

From disclosure categories to product labels

Since 2021 the EU's Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation (SFDR) has required fund managers to disclose how they treat sustainability. In practice, its Article 8 ("promotes environmental or social characteristics") and Article 9 ("sustainable investment objective") became used as quality labels — something they were never designed to be — and many managers excluded defence to fit them, even though the regulation did not require it.

In November 2025 the Commission proposed a revision, often called SFDR 2.0. It would replace Articles 8 and 9 with three voluntary product categories — Transition, ESG Basics and Sustainable — each with minimum criteria and exclusions, and it clarifies that SFDR should not be read as preventing investment in the defence sector (while keeping exclusions for controversial weapons). The Council adopted its position in June 2026 and the Parliament's ECON committee in September 2026; trilogue negotiations may conclude around the turn of 2026/27, with application not expected before late 2028 or 2029.

Part 3 · What changed

Why defence came back into the debate

War, budgets and policy

Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 changed the political context. In March 2025 the Commission presented the ReArm Europe Plan / Readiness 2030, aiming to enable up to around €800 billion of defence spending, including SAFE — up to €150 billion of EU-backed loans for joint procurement, with funding approved for 18 member states by April 2026. At the 2025 NATO summit in The Hague, allies agreed a target of 5% of GDP for defence and security-related spending by 2035.

Governments and regulators began to state openly that defence and sustainable investing are not incompatible. For investors, defence turned from a reputational risk into a policy-supported growth theme — which is also why it needs careful valuation rather than enthusiasm.

Neither column is trivially wrong. What matters for an investor is to decide explicitly, on stated principles, rather than to inherit a default.

Part 5 · Hard limits

Controversial weapons — where law decides

Not a matter of preference

Separate from the conventional-defence debate, some weapons are banned by international treaties: biological and chemical weapons, anti-personnel mines (Ottawa Convention) and cluster munitions (Oslo Convention). Several European countries go further and restrict the financing of companies involved in them; Switzerland's War Material Act, for example, prohibits the direct financing of the development or manufacture of prohibited war materiel. Nuclear weapons sit in a contested middle ground: banned by a treaty that most European states have not signed, yet excluded by many investors.

What to check
Revenue exposure. What share of a company's revenue comes from defence, and from which products.
Controversial weapons involvement. Data providers flag companies linked to banned weapons — check before buying.
Export markets. Who the customers are, and how export licences are controlled.
Order backlog vs. valuation. Policy-driven demand can be priced in long before it reaches earnings.
Regulatory change. The final SFDR 2.0 text and national rules may change what funds can hold.
Part 6 · My position

Defence in VB Europe Strategic

A stated choice, with one boundary

My portfolio treats defence as part of European self-sufficiency: a continent that wants to lead again needs to be able to protect itself, and its defence industry is part of the industrial base my thesis is about. That is why the statute does not exclude companies within the sector.

One clarification belongs in the statute: "no exclusions within defence" should mean conventional defence. Companies involved in weapons banned by international treaties are a different category, and financing them can be restricted by law. Writing that boundary down explicitly is part of taking the choice seriously.

Conclusion

Bringing it together

ESG is a family of approaches, not one rule. For most of the past decade, European sustainable funds excluded defence by default, partly because the SFDR's categories were used as labels. Since 2022 war, budgets and regulation have reopened the question, and the coming SFDR 2.0 explicitly states that it does not stand in the way of defence financing. The debate is legitimate on both sides; what an investor owes is an explicit, reasoned position — and respect for the legal limits on controversial weapons.
