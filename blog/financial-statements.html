How the Three Financial Statements Connect

Income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement — how each is built, and how one change ripples through the other two

Every company reports three statements, and most introductions explain them one at a time. That is exactly why they stay abstract. This article treats them as one connected system: we build a small company from scratch, record its transactions one by one, and watch each of them land in all three statements at once.

The bridge between the instruments and valuation: before putting a value on a share or a bond, you need to read the numbers behind it. Depth: roughly CFA Level I (Financial Statement Analysis).

Objective

I know that there are three financial statements. What I could not picture was how they are put together and, above all, how an increase or decrease in one of them turns into changes in the other two. Reading them separately, each one made sense; as a system, it was a black box. That is the gap this article sets out to close.

It matters because everything that follows depends on it. A DCF starts from cash flows that are derived from all three statements; a bond analyst checks whether operating cash covers interest; a portfolio manager has to tell a company whose profits are real from one whose profits exist only on paper. None of that works without understanding how the statements move together.

The one rule to hold on to

Assets = Liabilities + Equity, always. Every transaction touches at least two places, and after every transaction this equation still holds. If you can check that, you understand the system.

Part 1 · The building blocks

The three statements, one question each

A film, a photograph, and the bank statement
Income statement — "Did the company make a profit?" A film of one period (a quarter, a year). Revenue minus expenses gives net income. It follows accrual accounting, so it records what was earned and incurred, not what was paid.
Balance sheet — "What does it own and owe?" A photograph at one moment. Assets on one side; liabilities and shareholders' equity on the other. The two sides are equal by construction.
Cash flow statement — "Where did the cash come from and go?" The bank statement of the period, sorted into three buckets: operating (CFO, running the business), investing (CFI, buying and selling long-term assets) and financing (CFF, dealing with lenders and shareholders).

A fourth, smaller statement — the statement of changes in equity — explains how equity moved during the period: profit added, dividends paid, shares issued or bought back. It is the place where the income statement formally meets the balance sheet.

Part 2 · The reason for three statements

Why profit is not cash

Accrual accounting separates earning from getting paid

If every sale were paid immediately and every cost paid on the spot, profit and cash would be the same and one statement would be enough. In reality they drift apart, because accounting under IFRS and US GAAP follows the accrual principle: revenue is recognised when it is earned (the goods are delivered), and expenses when they are incurred — matched to the revenue they helped produce — regardless of when cash moves.

Three things create the gap. Timing: a sale on credit is revenue today and cash next month (a receivable); a supplier bill paid later is a cost today and cash outflow next month (a payable). Non-cash expenses: depreciation spreads the cost of a machine over its life, so profit falls every year while the cash left in the year the machine was bought. Balance-sheet-only flows: borrowing, repaying debt or buying equipment move cash without touching profit at all.

The balance sheet is where these gaps are parked — as receivables, inventory, payables, PP&E and debt. The cash flow statement is the reconciliation that explains, line by line, how profit became cash. That is the whole logic of the three-statement system.

Part 3 · The wiring

The links between the statements

Three bridges hold the system together

① Income statement → balance sheet. Net income does not vanish at the end of the year; whatever is not paid out as dividends is added to retained earnings in equity: REend = REbegin + Net income − Dividends. This is how profit becomes part of what shareholders own.

② Income statement → cash flow statement. Under the indirect method, used by most companies, CFO starts from net income and then undoes everything that was not cash: it adds back non-cash expenses such as depreciation and amortisation, and adjusts for changes in working capital.

③ Cash flow statement → balance sheet. Opening cash + net change in cash = closing cash, and that closing cash is exactly the cash line on the balance sheet. If they differ, something is missing.

④ Balance sheet → cash flow statement. The cash flow statement is, in effect, the explanation of how every balance-sheet line changed. Rules of thumb:

An asset (other than cash) goes up → cash goes down. Higher receivables mean sales not yet collected; more inventory means cash tied up in goods; more PP&E means capex was spent.
A liability or equity goes up → cash goes up. Higher payables mean bills not yet paid; new debt or new shares bring cash in.
And the reverse. Collecting receivables or selling assets brings cash in; paying suppliers, repaying debt, paying dividends or buying back shares takes it out.
IFRS vs. US GAAP — where a cash flow is classified
Under US GAAP, interest paid is operating and dividends paid are financing. IAS 7 has allowed IFRS companies to choose (interest paid in operating or financing; interest and dividends received in operating or investing). From 2027, IFRS 18 removes most of these choices for non-financial companies. When comparing CFO across companies, check where interest sits.
Part 4 · Learning by doing

Alpen Tools AG, a fictional company, in its first year — tax rate 20%

Check every bridge from Part 3. ① Retained earnings went from 0 to 15 = net income 20 − dividend 5. ② CFO starts with net income 20. ③ Opening cash 100 + change 27 = 127, the balance-sheet cash. ④ Each adjustment in CFO is a balance-sheet change: receivables rose by 20 (70 billed, 50 collected), inventory by 10 (40 bought, 30 sold), payables by 40 (nothing paid yet).

Notice what the numbers say. The company earned 20 of profit but generated 42 of operating cash — mainly because it has not yet paid its supplier. After the machine purchase, free cash flow (CFO − capex) was −18, and the business was funded by the bank. Profit alone would never have shown that.

A useful check: the CFO can also be built directly from the cash transactions — collected 50, paid interest 3, paid tax 5 → 42. The indirect and direct methods always give the same CFO; they only show the route differently.

Part 5 · The core skill

"What happens if…": one change, three statements

The classic exercise — trace a single change through the whole system (tax rate 20%)

The method is always the same: start with the income statement (does profit change?), move to the cash flow statement (start from the change in net income, then adjust for non-cash and balance-sheet effects), and finish on the balance sheet (cash from the cash flow statement, retained earnings from net income, plus any other line affected) — and check that both sides move by the same amount.

Worked in full: depreciation increases by 10

Income statement: EBIT −10 → taxable profit −10 → tax −2 → net income −8.

Cash flow statement: net income −8, but depreciation is added back +10 → CFO +2. Cash goes up, because the company pays 2 less in tax (the depreciation tax shield).

Balance sheet: cash +2, PP&E −10 → assets −8. Retained earnings −8 → equity −8. −8 = −8 ✓

Two patterns repeat. A non-cash expense (depreciation, write-downs, accruals) lowers profit but raises cash by the tax it saves. A balance-sheet-only transaction (inventory bought, debt-funded capex, collections) never touches profit but moves cash — which is precisely why the cash flow statement exists.

Simplification: tax is assumed to be paid in cash in the same period and book and tax depreciation are assumed equal. In reality, timing differences create deferred taxes — a later topic.

Part 6 · From mechanics to judgement

Reading statements as an investor

What the links reveal that a single statement hides

Once the links are clear, the statements become a tool for checking each other. Profit is shaped by accounting choices; cash is much harder to dress up. Comparing the two is the first test of earnings quality.

What to watch
CFO persistently below net income. Profits that never turn into cash deserve suspicion.
Receivables growing faster than revenue. Sales may be booked aggressively, or customers are paying later.
Inventory growing faster than sales. Demand may be weakening, or write-downs may be coming.
CFO boosted by stretching payables. Delaying suppliers lifts cash once, but it cannot be repeated forever.
Capex well below depreciation for years. The company may be under-investing to flatter free cash flow.
Growth funded by CFF. If operating cash cannot fund the business, it depends on lenders and shareholders.
Classification choices. Under IFRS, check where interest and dividends sit before comparing CFO.
In my portfolio

This is the foundation under every valuation I want to publish. A DCF does not value profit; it values free cash flow, which comes from all three statements at once: FCFF = CFO + interest × (1 − t) − capex, or built up from EBIT × (1 − t) + D&A − capex − Δworking capital. Conclusion · The system on one page

Bringing it together

The three statements are three views of the same set of transactions. The income statement measures performance on an accrual basis; the balance sheet stores the results and the timing differences; the cash flow statement reconciles profit with cash. Net income flows into retained earnings and opens the cash flow statement; the change in cash closes it and lands on the balance sheet; and every other balance-sheet change shows up as a cash flow. Trace any transaction through all three, check that assets still equal liabilities plus equity, and the black box opens.
