Macro and Central Banks

How growth, inflation and interest rates move markets — with the ECB and the SNB as the two central banks that matter most to me

Companies do not operate in a vacuum. Their sales depend on the economy, their valuations on interest rates, and a foreign investor's returns on exchange rates. This article connects the macro picture to asset prices, and looks at the two central banks behind my portfolio: the European Central Bank and the Swiss National Bank.

Objective

The sectors article showed that companies behave differently across the economic cycle. What drives the cycle — and how central banks steer it — was left open. For my portfolio this is not abstract: the ECB sets the cost of money for the companies I hold, the SNB for the currency I measure my results in, and the gap between the two moves the EUR/CHF exchange rate that sits on top of every return.

This article covers the main macro variables, how monetary policy works and reaches markets, where the ECB and SNB stand now, and what I should watch.

Part 1 · The dashboard

The macro variables that matter

Growth, inflation, labour, and the cycle
GDP growth — the size and pace of the economy; drives company revenues, especially in cyclical sectors.
Inflation — the rise in the general price level. Central banks in the euro area and Switzerland target price stability: 2% for the ECB, 0–2% for the SNB.
Labour market — employment and wages; strong wage growth can keep inflation high.
Leading indicators — PMIs, business and consumer confidence, the yield curve, credit spreads; they move before GDP does.

The business cycle moves through expansion, peak, contraction and trough. Markets move ahead of the data: equities usually fall before a recession is confirmed and recover before it ends. That is why investors watch leading indicators and central banks rather than last quarter's GDP.

Part 2 · The toolkit

How monetary policy works

Interest rates, balance sheets and the exchange rate

A central bank's main tool is its policy rate — the price at which banks can borrow from or deposit money with it. In the euro area there are three key rates: the deposit facility rate (DFR, which in today's environment of ample liquidity is the one that steers market rates), the main refinancing rate and the marginal lending rate. Raising rates cools demand and inflation; cutting them supports growth.

Beyond rates, central banks use their balance sheets: buying bonds (quantitative easing) lowers long-term yields, letting bonds mature without replacing them (quantitative tightening) does the opposite. The ECB also has a tool (TPI) to counter unwarranted widening of government bond spreads. The SNB, running a small open economy with a safe-haven currency, also uses foreign-exchange interventions — selling francs to stop the currency from strengthening too much, or buying them back.

Part 3 · The euro area

The ECB now

An energy shock and a rate hike

After cutting rates from mid-2024 to a deposit rate of 2.00% by June 2025, the ECB reversed course in 2026. The war in the Middle East pushed energy prices and inflation up, and in June 2026 the Governing Council raised rates for the first time since 2023, taking the deposit rate to 2.25%. A second 25-basis-point hike followed in September, to 2.50% (effective 16 September), with the Council saying inflation was set to stay well above target for an extended period. At its meeting on 29 October 2026 it held rates unchanged and did not pre-commit to a path.

On the same day it was confirmed that President Christine Lagarde will step down early, on 7 February 2027, about nine months before her term ends. Who succeeds her — and how hawkish that person is — becomes a market variable in its own right.

Part 4 · Switzerland

The SNB now

Zero rates and a watchful eye on the franc

The Swiss National Bank has held its policy rate at 0% since June 2025, the lowest among major central banks, and kept it there in September 2026. Its conditional inflation forecast was about 0.7% for 2026 and 0.8% for 2027 and 2028 — inside its 0–2% range — and it repeated that it is willing to intervene in the foreign-exchange market if needed. Banks' sight deposits above a threshold are remunerated 0.25 percentage points below the policy rate.

The result is a widening rate gap: euro rates at 2.50%, Swiss rates at zero. Rate differentials are one driver of EUR/CHF, alongside safe-haven flows that tend to push the franc up in times of stress.

Part 5 · My portfolio

What I watch for VB Europe Strategic

Turning macro into a checklist
Macro checklist
ECB path and the new president. Further hikes would weigh on long-duration and leveraged holdings, and support banks.
Energy prices. The main driver of the current inflation shock and of margins in industry.
EUR/CHF. A weaker euro cuts my CHF returns even when the shares rise; SNB interventions and safe-haven flows matter.
Sovereign spreads. BTP–Bund widening signals stress in the euro area.
Fiscal policy. Defence and infrastructure spending shift earnings towards industrials and defence.
Leading indicators. Eurozone PMIs and the yield curve for the direction of the cycle.

I do not want to trade on macro forecasts — that is a different strategy. The goal is to understand which holdings are most exposed to which macro risk, so the portfolio is not unintentionally one big bet on rates or on the euro.

Conclusion

Bringing it together

Macro variables set the backdrop, and central banks are the most powerful actors in it. Their rates feed into discount rates, earnings, bank margins, exchange rates and risk appetite. In late 2026 the ECB is tightening against an energy shock while the SNB holds at zero — a divergence that matters directly for a Swiss-franc investor in European equities.
