Behavioural Finance

Why investors — including me — make predictable mistakes, and how to build a process that guards against them

Classical finance assumes rational investors. Real investors feel losses more than gains, hold on to losers, trade too much and trust their own judgement too far. This article covers the main biases, the evidence behind them, and the practical defences I want to build into my own portfolio.

Depth: roughly CFA Level I (Portfolio Management — behavioural biases). Written before the real-money phase of VB Europe Strategic, to be revisited after it.

Objective

One reason I plan to invest real money in my portfolio, not just paper money, is to learn behavioural finance from the inside. On paper, every rule is easy to follow. With my own money at stake, the same rules will feel very different — and the research says exactly how they will feel different.

So this article is written before that phase: what the biases are, where the evidence comes from, which ones are most dangerous for a strategy like mine, and which defences I put in place in advance. After the live phase I want to come back and compare.

Part 1 · The foundation

From rational investors to prospect theory

How people actually weigh gains and losses

Traditional finance assumes investors are rational, risk-averse and update beliefs correctly. Behavioural finance, built on the work of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, shows systematic departures from that. Their prospect theory (1979) describes three regularities: people judge outcomes as gains and losses relative to a reference point (often the purchase price), not as final wealth; they are loss-averse — a loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain pleases; and they are risk-averse for gains but risk-seeking for losses, preferring to gamble rather than accept a sure loss.

These regularities are not random errors that cancel out across investors. They are predictable, which is why they can move prices and why they can be guarded against.

Part 2 · The catalogue

The main biases

Cognitive errors and emotional biases

The CFA curriculum splits biases into two groups. Cognitive errors come from faulty reasoning and can often be corrected with better information and process. Emotional biases come from feelings and are harder to fix; they usually have to be accommodated with rules.

Confirmation biasCognitiveSeeking information that supports the thesis, discounting what contradicts it
AnchoringCognitiveSticking to an initial number — the purchase price, a past target — despite new information
RepresentativenessCognitiveJudging by resemblance: a good company must be a good stock

Regret aversion and herdingEmotionalFollowing the crowd so that mistakes are shared
Part 3 · The evidence

What the research shows

Biases measured in real portfolios
The disposition effect — investors sell winners too early and hold losers too long. Shefrin and Statman named it in 1985; Odean (1998) confirmed it in thousands of brokerage accounts, and found that the winners sold went on to outperform the losers kept.
Overtrading — Barber and Odean (2000) found that the households that traded most earned markedly lower net returns than those that traded least. Activity felt like skill; it was mostly cost.
Home bias — French and Poterba (1991) documented that investors hold far more domestic stocks than diversification would justify.
Momentum and overreaction — patterns such as short-term momentum and long-term reversal are often explained by investors under- and over-reacting to news.
A bias close to home
A portfolio built only from European companies by a European investor is, by construction, open to the charge of home bias. My answer is that it is an explicit thematic strategy, not an accidental default — but that distinction only holds if the statute says so and the results are compared honestly with a broader benchmark.
Part 4 · Defences

Building a process that protects me from myself

Rules decided in calm conditions

Knowing about biases does little on its own; experienced investors show them too. What works is process — decisions made in advance, written down, and checked afterwards:

My defences
A written statute. Objective, universe, weights, rebalancing and sell rules fixed before the first trade — an investment policy statement for myself.
A decision journal. For every trade: the thesis, the expected outcome, what would prove me wrong, and my emotional state. Reviewed later against what happened.
Pre-defined sell criteria. Sell when the thesis breaks, not because of the purchase price. This targets the disposition effect directly.
A devil's-advocate section. For each holding, the strongest case against it — a check on confirmation bias.
Rebalancing by calendar. Fixed dates rather than reactions to headlines, which limits overtrading.
Fixed benchmark and metrics. Decided in advance, so I cannot choose the flattering comparison afterwards.
Part 5 · My portfolio

What I will track in the live phase

Turning the journal into data

When real money is in the portfolio, the decision journal becomes a dataset. I want to track, for each trade, whether I followed the statute or deviated from it, how long I held winners compared with losers, how often I traded compared with the plan, and how the deviations performed. At the end of the project, that comparison — what the rules said versus what I actually did — will be the most honest part of the reflection.

Conclusion

Bringing it together

Behavioural finance replaces the rational investor with a real one: loss-averse, overconfident, anchored to reference points and prone to follow the crowd. The biases are well documented and predictable, and they cost money — through holding losers, overtrading and under-diversifying. The defence is not willpower but process: rules written in calm conditions, a journal that records decisions as they happen, and an honest review of the gap between the two.
