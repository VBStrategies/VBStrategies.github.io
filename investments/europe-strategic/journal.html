14 October 2026ResearchLearning Where to start: the method before the companies Before the first company enters the funnel, I need two tools: a way to value a business and a way to write about it. I start with valuation. ›

The statute sets the direction. The next question is practical: what do I do on day one with the first company? According to the selection process, every company gets a one-page thesis, an equity research report and an estimate of its intrinsic value. Before I can do that well and the same way every time, I need to settle the method.

Two building blocks

  • Valuation: how to estimate intrinsic value. The Roche DCF was one model. There are many more: dividend discount, residual income, sum of the parts, net asset value, and relative valuation with trading and transaction comparables. I want to map all of them, with when each one fits and when it misleads.
  • Writing: the one-page thesis and the equity research report. A fixed structure, so every company is analysed and presented the same way and can be compared.

Why valuation first

Valuation is the less abstract of the two for me. I have already built one model, and the report structure partly depends on it: the valuation section is the heart of every report. Once I know which methods I use and why, the template follows.

Where it will live

Both guides are general, so they go to the Blog, not here. The rule I am setting: the Blog explains how something is done; On Investments shows it applied. Each research report will link back to the guide it follows.

Next: a blog post on company valuation, covering every main model, when to use it, and how comparables fit in. Then the thesis and report template.

13 October 2026StatuteLearning Statute v1.0: from first draft to rules What changed from the first draft and why, how fundamental and statistical methods fit together, and the honest caveats. ›

The first draft of the statute was a list of my own ideas. Turning it into rules meant cutting the personal parts, replacing some limits with method and moving everything that is not policy here.

Fundamental or statistical? Both.

At school we learn mostly the statistical approach to portfolio management. My plan was fundamental analysis. The answer is to combine them in a funnel: Europe, then sectors, then companies with real added value, which is the fundamental part. Then the weights come from statistics.

The idea came from a discussion with my teacher. Don't buy 40 selected companies equally. Measure how they move together. If ten of them behave the same way, statistics tells you to cut their combined weight and give room to the companies that balance the risk.

The link between both worlds is the Black–Litterman model: my valuations become "views" that adjust the market's implied returns, and the optimiser turns them into weights.

Under consideration, not in the statute

  • Currency hedging with derivatives. Holdings in CHF, SEK or NOK add currency risk to a EUR portfolio. A currency forward fixes today's exchange rate for a future date; its cost or gain comes from the interest rate difference between the two currencies. I don't know this well enough yet, so it stays out of version 1.0.
  • Country and policy monitoring. A regular note on national and EU politics that affect holdings: elections, defence budgets, energy and industrial policy. Whether it belongs in the quarterly report or in the risk process is still open.
  • Tools. Excel for valuation, R (RStudio) for statistics, OpenBB as an open-source terminal, plus free data: company reports, Yahoo Finance, Koyfin, Damodaran's datasets, the ECB data portal.

Honest caveats

  • A backtest cannot fully test stock picking. I cannot re-create, without hindsight, the valuations I would have made five years ago. The backtest fairly tests the universe rules and the weighting method; stock picking is tested only in the live stages.
  • Survivorship bias. A historical universe must include companies that later failed or were delisted, or the results look better than they were.
  • Optimisers amplify estimation errors. Mean-variance optimisation tends to put large weights on the assets whose returns are most overestimated. Shrinkage, constraints and the equal-weight baseline are the defence.
  • Small caps cost more to trade. Thinner trading and wider spreads can make real results worse than the backtest assumes.

9 October 2026Strategy Why a strategy, not a stock How the Roche model led to a portfolio, why Europe, how the pillars were chosen and why the UK is out. ›

The Roche DCF showed me that a single valuation means little until you know what it is compared against and what role it plays in a portfolio. So instead of the next model, I started with the frame that every future model will plug into.

Why now. I am starting my master's and want to turn what I learn into practice, to see how the world of asset management really works. I also simply enjoy it.

Why Europe. Europe, once a shaper of the world economy, is now sometimes called dead. I want to find the right firms to invest in and so support its growth. The Draghi report and NATO's 5% commitment turn that ambition into concrete spending, and someone has to supply it.

How the pillars were chosen. By asking what Europe must build for itself: security, energy, technology, industry and health. Private equity was added as a sixth component to reach companies that are not yet listed. Defence has no sub-sector exclusions, and healthcare counts as a growth pillar, not a defensive add-on.

The UK is out. I decided to leave the UK out of the universe, so the benchmark is MSCI Europe ex UK instead of the STOXX Europe 600, which carries a large UK weight.

In three years I want to know where every step led: where I made mistakes and where I succeeded. This journal exists for exactly that.
